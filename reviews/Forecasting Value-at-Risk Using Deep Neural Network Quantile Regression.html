Forecasting Value-at-Risk Using Deep Neural Network Quantile Regression

Probabilistic forecasting
Quantile regression
Value-at-Risk
Deep learning
Explainable AI
Finance
Review of a Journal of Financial Econometrics paper proposing deep neural network quantile regression for non-linear Value-at-Risk forecasting, with mixed-frequency extensions and interpretability via SHAP and partial derivatives.
Published

March 13, 2026

  • Authors & Year: Ilias Chronopoulos, Aristeidis Raftapostolos, and George Kapetanios (2024 journal version; advance access 2023)
  • Venue (Journal/Conference): Journal of Financial Econometrics
  • DOI: 10.1093/jjfinec/nbad014
  • Primary Topic: Deep neural network quantile regression for non-linear VaR forecasting
  • Secondary Topics: Mixed-frequency modeling, MIDAS, Monte Carlo validation, interpretability of quantile neural networks, SHAP, partial derivatives
  • Code/Data Available?: Not emphasized in the core paper text I reviewed
  • Proximity/Threat Level: Medium to High, especially if my work involves deep quantile forecasting, explainability, or benchmarking non-linear quantile models
  • Date Reviewed: 2026-03-13

2. Core Contributions, Novelty & Motivation

The Motivation (Why Quantiles / Why VaR?)

  • The paper starts from the classical argument that mean regression is often inadequate when the real interest is in the conditional tails of a distribution.
  • In finance, this matters because risk management is fundamentally about downside extremes rather than average behavior.
  • Value-at-Risk (VaR) is directly linked to conditional quantiles of returns, so a quantile regression framework is a natural modeling choice.
  • The authors argue that standard linear quantile regression may be too restrictive for financial markets, where relationships between returns and predictors are often non-linear, asymmetric, and unstable over time.

The Motivation (Why Deep Learning?)

  • The central justification is that feed-forward neural networks have strong universal approximation properties, so they can approximate highly non-linear conditional quantile functions much more flexibly than linear or low-order sieve approaches.
  • They position neural networks not just as generic predictors, but as a way to estimate a conditional quantile function directly through the quantile loss.

The Motivation (Why Explainability?)

  • The authors explicitly acknowledge the “black-box” criticism of neural networks.
  • Their response is not merely to forecast better, but also to make the neural quantile model more interpretable through:
    • SHAP values, and
    • partial derivatives of the fitted network output with respect to inputs.
  • This is important because in risk forecasting, users want to know not only what the VaR forecast is, but also why it changed.

Their claim to fame / Novelty

  • They propose a deep quantile estimator that directly models non-linear conditional quantiles via feed-forward neural networks.
  • They extend the setup to allow mixed-frequency inputs through a MIDAS-style architecture.
  • They also contribute to explainable ML in finance by comparing SHAP with partial-derivative-based interpretation.
  • The paper frames itself as, to the best of the authors’ knowledge, the first finance application using a neural network quantile estimator specifically for VaR forecasting.

Their ultimate result

  • The deep quantile estimator outperforms linear quantile, MIDAS, and other non-parametric quantile competitors in their VaR forecasting exercise.
  • They report predictive gains of up to 98% relative to the linear quantile benchmark, and up to 84% for the non-linear MIDAS model, depending on the setup.
  • Their interpretability comparison also concludes that partial derivatives are more stable and much cheaper computationally than SHAP, while still being informative.

3. Methodology & Technical Details

Core modeling idea

  • Standard quantile regression estimates the conditional quantile by minimizing the pinball / check loss.
  • The authors replace the linear predictor (x_t^()) with a neural network (G_(x_t; w)), yielding a non-linear quantile regression estimator: [ Q_y(x_t) = G_(x_t; w) ]
  • The model is trained by minimizing the quantile loss over the neural network parameters.

Network structure

  • They focus on feed-forward neural networks.
  • Hidden layers use ReLU activations.
  • The output layer is linear.
  • They frame the model as ranging from relatively parametric to more non-parametric depending on network width/depth.

Regularization

To control overfitting, they consider: - No regularization - Deep LASSO - Deep Ridge - Deep Elastic Net

This is a strong part of the paper because they do not present “deep learning” as a single monolithic model; they explicitly examine how different penalties affect finite-sample behavior and forecast quality.

Hyperparameter tuning

They use a time-series cross-validation framework rather than random CV, which is the correct choice for dependent data.

Tuned components include: - number of hidden layers - number of neurons - learning rate - batch size - dropout - regularization strength - Elastic Net mixing parameter - MIDAS polynomial parameters for mixed-frequency models

Optimization

  • They use ADAM
  • Maximum epochs: 5000
  • Early stopping is used to reduce overfitting

4. Monte Carlo Design & What It Actually Shows

Why the Monte Carlo section matters

This is one of the more valuable parts of the paper.
The authors do not jump straight to empirical finance results. They first ask whether the proposed deep quantile estimator behaves sensibly in controlled settings.

Monte Carlo setup

They study finite-sample performance over: - quantiles ( {1%, 2.5%, 5%, 10%, 20%} ) - sample sizes ( T {100, 300, 500, 1000} ) - 1000 replications

They compare: - deep quantile - deep LASSO - deep Ridge - deep Elastic Net - shallow counterparts - linear quantile regression - polynomial quantile regression - spline-based quantile regression

Simulation cases

They test five settings: 1. Simple non-linear sinusoidal function with i.i.d. Gaussian regressor 2. AR(1) regressor 3. GARCH-type regressor 4. A function generated from a neural-network-like composition 5. Serially correlated errors

What they find

  • Both shallow and deep neural quantile estimators generally show good finite-sample performance
  • Shallow networks often outperform deeper ones in the simulation study
  • Linear quantile regression fails badly when the true quantile function is non-linear
  • Polynomial and spline alternatives also generally underperform in these non-linear settings
  • Penalization is often helpful, especially in more difficult settings and extreme quantiles

My interpretation of this section

This Monte Carlo exercise gives the paper real methodological credibility.
It is not merely “deep learning won on one dataset.” The authors show that the model class itself is able to recover non-linear quantile functions under several stylized but meaningful data-generating processes.

That said, the simulations are still relatively low-dimensional and controlled. They do not stress-test the method under severe regime change, covariate shift, heavy tails beyond Gaussian innovations, or highly multivariate predictor spaces of the kind now common in financial ML.

5. Empirical Application: VaR Forecasting Setup

Target problem

  • Forecast 10-day-ahead VaR using daily data
  • U.S. stock return losses over a long historical sample
  • Sample period discussed in the paper: September 1985 to August 2020

Out-of-sample design

  • Last 2000 observations reserved for out-of-sample evaluation
  • Remaining 7053 observations used for tuning and estimation
  • This split is chosen partly to make the conditional quantile forecast encompassing results comparable with earlier literature

Quantiles forecasted

  • ( = 1%, 5%, 10% )

VaR model classes used as inputs / structures

They examine four VaR specifications: 1. GARCH(1,1)-style VaR 2. RiskMetrics 3. CAViaR symmetric absolute value (SV) 4. CAViaR asymmetric slope (ASV)

Then they build: - linear quantile versions - MIDAS versions - non-linear deep quantile versions - non-linear deep MIDAS versions

What is important here

This is not a totally unconstrained “throw raw data into a neural net” paper.
The authors still organize the forecasting problem around well-known VaR structures from the econometrics literature. In that sense, the paper is trying to bridge: - classical risk econometrics, and - modern non-linear function approximation.

That makes it much stronger than a purely black-box finance paper.

6. Validation Strategy (Predictions & Explanations)

Validating the predictions

They use several forecast-comparison tools: - Diebold–Mariano (DM) test - Giacomini–White (GW) test - quantile score test - conditional quantile forecast encompassing test (Giacomini and Komunjer framework)

What they conclude from these tests

  • The deep quantile estimator generally has higher forecasting accuracy
  • It often encompasses competing forecasts more often than the reverse
  • In some cases, the encompassing tests are inconclusive, suggesting that forecast combinations may outperform any single model

Validating the explanations

This is much less rigorous.

They compare: - SHAP values - partial derivatives

And judge them on: - interpretability - stability over time - computational cost - ability to reflect known stress periods like the 2008 financial crisis and COVID-19

This is useful, but it is still mostly qualitative validation rather than a formal XAI evaluation framework.

7. Explainability Contribution: What Did XAI Actually Do?

Their two interpretation tools

  1. SHAP values
    • Used as a standard post-hoc feature attribution framework
  2. Partial derivatives
    • Used to examine marginal sensitivity of the predicted quantile to each input

Their main interpretability findings

  • Partial derivatives are more stable over time
  • Partial derivatives are far cheaper computationally than SHAP
  • The derivative-based interpretation fluctuates around the linear quantile estimate and can capture time variation linked to major market stress events

Why this matters

This is an interesting contribution because most finance explainability papers default to SHAP and stop there.
These authors ask whether a simpler structural sensitivity measure may actually be more practical.

My interpretation

The paper’s most interesting XAI insight is not that SHAP works, but that SHAP may not be the most useful interpretability tool in this setting.
For continuously differentiable models, partial derivatives can provide: - a faster, - more stable, - more directly sensitivity-based view of model behavior.

That is a very useful takeaway for any forecasting paper where interpretability has to be repeated over many rolling windows or over long historical horizons.

8. Main Strengths of the Paper

Strength 1: It is methodologically balanced

The paper combines: - theory motivation, - simulation evidence, - empirical forecasting, - and interpretability.

That gives it a more complete arc than many ML-for-finance papers.

Strength 2: It respects time-series structure

Their use of: - time-series cross-validation, - proper out-of-sample design, - formal forecast comparison tests, makes the work much stronger than papers that only report in-sample fit or random train/test splits.

Strength 3: It benchmarks against econometric baselines

The authors do not compare only against strawman mean models.
They include: - linear quantile models, - MIDAS variants, - CAViaR-type structures, - and non-parametric alternatives.

Strength 4: It gives an actual interpretability discussion

Even if not fully rigorous, the paper takes the interpretability problem seriously and tests two different tools rather than treating explainability as an afterthought.

Strength 5: It explicitly studies regularization

The paper acknowledges that neural quantile estimators can overfit, and it studies how penalties change performance. That is a mature methodological move.

9. My Thoughts, Criticisms & Unstated Limitations

Criticism 1: The paper still uses a fairly narrow input structure

Although the authors motivate high-dimensional flexibility, the actual VaR specifications remain relatively low-dimensional and structurally tied to traditional VaR equations.

This is not necessarily bad, but it means the paper does not fully demonstrate how the method behaves in genuinely high-dimensional financial forecasting environments with many covariates, macro variables, realized measures, order-book features, or alternative data.

Criticism 2: The interpretability evaluation is still mostly qualitative

The comparison between SHAP and partial derivatives is interesting, but it is not validated with: - faithfulness metrics, - stability metrics, - perturbation tests, - agreement under resampling, - or formal sensitivity benchmarks.

So while the conclusion that partial derivatives are “better” is plausible and practically useful, it is not fully established in a rigorous XAI-evaluation sense.

Criticism 3: The forecasting victory may partly reflect flexible function approximation rather than a uniquely finance-specific insight

The deep quantile model wins because it can represent non-linear conditional quantiles better than linear models.
That is a valid result, but it is also somewhat unsurprising.
The paper is stronger as a demonstration of the value of non-linear quantile function estimation than as a finance-theory breakthrough.

Criticism 4: The deep-vs-shallow message is awkward

In Monte Carlo, shallow models often look better than deeper ones, yet the paper is framed around “deep” quantile regression. That does not invalidate the work, but it does weaken any simplistic narrative that more depth is inherently better.

Criticism 5: No broader uncertainty discussion beyond quantiles

This paper is about quantile forecasting, not full probabilistic density estimation.
That is fine, but it means it does not engage with: - calibration of full predictive distributions, - tail dependence structures, - extreme value theory integration, - or whether the estimated quantiles remain coherent across levels.

Criticism 6: Potential monotonicity / quantile crossing issues are not the main focus

Whenever multiple quantiles are estimated, one wants to know whether the resulting quantile curves are coherent and non-crossing.
This paper does not foreground that issue as a central methodological challenge.

Criticism 7: The benchmark set is respectable, but not modern by 2026 standards

For historical context the benchmark suite is reasonable.
But by current standards, one would also want comparisons against: - quantile gradient boosting, - quantile random forests, - distributional boosting, - modern sequence models, - conformalized quantile methods, - and perhaps heavy-tail parametric or semi-parametric baselines.

10. What This Paper Means for My Own Research

If my work is on probabilistic forecasting

This paper is highly relevant because it provides a concrete example of: - replacing linear quantile structure with a neural quantile function, - validating it in simulation, - and comparing it with conventional baselines.

If my work is on explainable forecasting

The most useful takeaway may actually be the partial-derivative vs SHAP comparison.
That gives me a strong precedent for arguing that: - SHAP is not the only route to explainability, - and in some forecasting settings, derivative-based explanations may be more stable and computationally realistic.

If my work is on energy forecasting rather than finance

Even though the domain is financial VaR, the methodological structure is transferable: - tail-focused forecasting, - quantile loss, - neural non-linearity, - time-series CV, - benchmarking against linear and structured baselines, - and interpretability of conditional quantile dynamics.

This is especially relevant if I want to forecast: - reserve requirements, - net-load risk, - renewable underproduction risk, - demand peaks, - or other lower/upper tail events.

11. How I Would Position This Paper in a Literature Review

As a foundational citation for neural quantile forecasting

I would cite it as evidence that: - neural networks can be used to estimate conditional quantiles directly, - and that doing so can materially improve tail-risk forecasting relative to linear quantile models.

As a bridge paper

It is best positioned as a bridge between: - classical econometric quantile/VaR methods, and - modern ML non-linear function approximation.

As an explainability citation

I would also use it when arguing that: - post-hoc explainability in probabilistic forecasting is possible, - but not all explainability tools are equally stable or practical.

As a limitation benchmark

I would contrast my own work against it by noting: - it focuses on VaR quantiles rather than full predictive distributions, - uses mostly qualitative explainability validation, - and does not fully explore modern high-dimensional or deep sequence architectures.

13. Action Items & Integration

14. Bottom-Line Review

This is a strong and thoughtful paper that does more than just apply a neural network to a finance problem.
Its main contribution is to show that direct neural quantile estimation can materially improve VaR forecasting when the conditional quantile function is non-linear, and that this can be studied in a reasonably disciplined way through simulation, out-of-sample testing, and interpretability analysis.

Its most interesting conceptual contribution is not merely “deep learning beats linear models,” but rather: 1. conditional quantiles are often non-linear,
2. neural networks provide a practical estimator for them, and
3. partial-derivative-based explanations may be a more stable alternative to SHAP in this context.

The paper’s main weaknesses are that the interpretability validation remains mostly qualitative, the input space is not especially high-dimensional in practice, and the benchmark set, while solid, is no longer exhaustive by modern standards.

Still, as a literature-review source, it is highly useful and deserves to be treated as an important reference for any research sitting at the intersection of: - quantile forecasting, - tail-risk prediction, - neural non-linear regression, - and explainable probabilistic modeling.
