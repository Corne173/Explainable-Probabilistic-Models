How to evaluate uncertainty estimates in machine learning for regression?

2. Core Contributions, Novelty & Motivation

What problem are they solving?

The paper argues that common ways of evaluating uncertainty estimates in regression can make an uncertainty method look reliable even when its intervals behave poorly in important regions of the input space.

The authors focus on two dominant evaluation habits:

  1. Log-likelihood testing, where models that output predictive densities are compared using test-set log-likelihood.
  2. Prediction interval coverage probability (PICP), where interval methods are judged by the fraction of test observations falling inside their intervals.

Both seem reasonable at first glance. If a predictive distribution assigns high likelihood to held-out observations, it sounds like the uncertainty is good. If a 90% interval contains about 90% of held-out observations, it sounds calibrated. The paper’s main point is that this reasoning is incomplete.

Main argument

The central claim is that aggregate predictive metrics do not prove that uncertainty estimates are locally reliable.

A method can:

  • achieve good PICP while being overconfident in one region and underconfident in another;
  • achieve better log-likelihood because its mean prediction is better, not because its uncertainty estimate is better;
  • produce acceptable predictive uncertainty while estimating model uncertainty and data noise variance incorrectly;
  • fail to widen confidence intervals in sparse or poorly sampled regions even though such widening is exactly what epistemic uncertainty should do.

What is novel?

The novelty is not a new forecasting model. The contribution is an evaluation framework.

The authors propose testing uncertainty estimates through controlled simulations where:

  • the data-generating function is known;
  • training data can be resampled repeatedly;
  • prediction and confidence intervals can be evaluated across repeated experiments;
  • coverage can be checked point by point rather than only on average.

This lets them evaluate pointwise coverage, not merely marginal or average coverage.

Why this matters

For probabilistic forecasting, it is tempting to report one coverage number and one sharpness number. This paper is useful because it explains why that is not enough. A model can have the right global PICP and still be unreliable in the operating regimes where uncertainty matters most.

For energy, load, solar, wind, or other operational forecasting problems, this is especially important because data are not uniformly informative across the system. Some regions may be well sampled, while others may be sparse, seasonal, shifted, or operationally rare. Those are precisely the regions where uncertainty estimates must behave cautiously.

3. Uncertainty Framework & Key Definitions

Regression setup

The paper works in a regression setting where observations are represented as:

[ Y X = x N(f(x), ^2(x)) ]

or equivalently:

[ y_i = f(x_i) + _i ]

Here:

  • (f(x)) is the true regression function;
  • (^2(x)) is the data noise variance;
  • ((x)) is the learned model estimate.

The authors distinguish two uncertainty components:

  • Model uncertainty: uncertainty about the learned estimate ((x)). This corresponds closely to epistemic uncertainty and should be high where the model has limited knowledge.
  • Data noise variance: irreducible noise in the observation process. This corresponds closely to aleatoric uncertainty.

The predictive uncertainty combines both components. That is part of the problem: a good total predictive uncertainty estimate can hide a bad decomposition into model uncertainty and data noise variance.

Prediction intervals vs confidence intervals

The paper is careful about the difference between prediction intervals and confidence intervals.

Prediction interval (PI):

  • aims to contain a future observation (Y);
  • includes both model uncertainty and data noise;
  • can be checked in real data by asking whether held-out observations fall inside the interval.

Confidence interval (CI):

  • aims to contain the true function value (f(x));
  • is about the uncertainty in the estimated function, not the noise in individual future observations;
  • cannot normally be checked directly in real-world data because (f(x)) is unknown.

This distinction is important. In ordinary applied modelling, if we check whether observed test values fall inside intervals, we are evaluating prediction interval coverage, not confidence interval coverage. Confidence interval coverage can only be evaluated directly when the true function is known, such as in simulation.

Pointwise vs marginal coverage

The most important distinction in the paper is between pointwise and marginal coverage.

Marginal coverage means the interval has the correct coverage on average over the input distribution.

Example: a 90% prediction interval covers 90% of test observations overall.

Pointwise coverage means the interval has the correct coverage at each input location (x).

Example: for each specific operating condition (x), the 90% interval covers the target 90% of the time across repeated experiments.

Pointwise coverage is stronger and more useful. Marginal coverage can hide local failures. A model can be too narrow in some regions and too wide in others, while still averaging to the right global PICP.

Important wording clarification

The paper often talks about testing coverage on a “single data set.” That wording can be confusing in applied system modelling.

The more useful interpretation is:

A single collected sample or single train/test split gives only one realization of the system’s data-generating process. It may not reveal whether intervals behave correctly across repeated samples or across all operating regimes of the system.

For a real physical or operational system, the concern is not just “one dataset” in the abstract. The concern is uneven system coverage. If some part of the operating space has little data, a model should express higher epistemic uncertainty there. A global PICP score can miss exactly that failure.

4. Existing Evaluation Strategies Critiqued

PICP-based interval testing

PICP is defined as the fraction of test observations that fall inside their prediction intervals:

[ PICP = _i 1{y_i PI(x_i)} ]

This is widely used because it is simple and intuitive. If we construct 90% intervals, we hope PICP is close to 0.9.

The paper’s criticism is that PICP estimates coverage averaged over the test distribution. It does not guarantee that intervals are reliable at each individual value of (x).

CICP-based confidence interval testing

The analogous confidence interval measure is CICP:

[ CICP = _i 1{f(x_i) CI(x_i)} ]

But this requires knowing the true function (f(x)). In normal real-world applications, we do not know this. That is why CICP is mainly available in simulation studies.

This is one of the key practical frustrations with the paper: the strongest evaluation target is not directly observable in ordinary applied modelling.

Log-likelihood testing

The log-likelihood procedure applies when a model outputs a full predictive density (p(y x)), often a normal distribution with estimated mean and variance. The method is then evaluated using average test-set log-likelihood.

This paper is not about a formal likelihood ratio test. It is about using log-likelihood as a predictive scoring metric to compare probabilistic models.

The authors acknowledge that log-likelihood is sensible if the goal is to estimate the full predictive density. However, they argue that if the practical goal is to construct good prediction intervals, a better log-likelihood does not necessarily imply better intervals.

Standard benchmark habit

The paper traces much of the benchmark culture to the probabilistic backpropagation evaluation setup, where methods are tested on regression datasets such as:

  • Boston Housing
  • Concrete Compression Strength
  • Energy Efficiency
  • Kin8nm
  • Naval Propulsion
  • Combined Cycle Power Plant
  • Protein Structure
  • Wine Quality Red
  • Yacht Hydrodynamics
  • Year Prediction MSD

The authors argue that these benchmarks are useful for comparison, but the usual metrics do not isolate uncertainty quality well enough.

5. Major Criticisms of Existing Evaluation

Criticism 1: Predictive performance does not prove correct uncertainty decomposition

Predictive uncertainty combines model uncertainty and data noise variance.

A method might get a good overall predictive uncertainty score because:

  • its model uncertainty estimate is good;
  • its data noise estimate is good;
  • one bad estimate compensates for another bad estimate;
  • the mean prediction is better, even though uncertainty is not.

Therefore, PICP and log-likelihood do not tell us whether epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty have been estimated correctly.

This matters for out-of-distribution detection. If model uncertainty is supposed to rise in unfamiliar regions, we need to know whether the model uncertainty component itself is behaving correctly. A good predictive score may not answer that.

Criticism 2: Coverage cannot be properly tested from one realization

Intervals are random because they depend on:

  • which training data were sampled;
  • which targets were observed;
  • random effects in model training, such as initialization and stochastic optimization.

To test whether an interval procedure has the desired coverage, the whole experiment should be repeated: sample data, train model, construct intervals, evaluate coverage.

The authors demonstrate this with a simple linear example. Even when the interval procedure is correctly calibrated, a single evaluation can look misleading. In one simulation, CICP may be 0; in another, it may be 1. This does not mean the interval procedure is useless. It means one realization cannot establish coverage.

Practical translation: cross-validation, bootstrapping, blocked resampling, repeated train/test splits, or simulation-based stress tests are attempts to approximate this repeated-experiment logic. They still do not give true CICP unless (f(x)) is known, but they are better than relying on one split.

Criticism 3: Good PICP does not guarantee pointwise coverage

PICP measures average coverage across the test set. It does not show where coverage succeeds or fails.

A model can achieve near-perfect PICP by:

  • over-covering easy or well-sampled regions;
  • under-covering difficult, sparse, shifted, or high-risk regions;
  • averaging those failures into a respectable global number.

This is one of the most important ideas to carry into forecasting work. The right question is not only:

Do the 90% intervals cover 90% of observations overall?

It is also:

Do the 90% intervals cover approximately 90% of observations in the operating regimes where decisions will be made?

Criticism 4: Better log-likelihood does not imply better prediction intervals

Log-likelihood rewards the whole predictive density. A model can improve log-likelihood because its mean predictions are better, even if its uncertainty estimate is worse.

The paper gives a small example where one method has slightly better log-likelihood, about -3.109 versus -3.110, but much worse 68% interval coverage, about 0.57 versus 0.67.

The takeaway is simple: if the final use of the model is interval construction, interval behaviour must be evaluated directly. Log-likelihood alone is not enough.

6. Proposed Solution: Simulation-Based Testing

Main idea

The authors propose controlled simulation-based evaluation.

Instead of relying on one real train/test split, the evaluator defines a data-generating process where the true function and noise process are known. Then the full modelling procedure is repeated many times.

This allows the evaluator to ask:

  • Do prediction intervals cover future observations at each (x)?
  • Do confidence intervals cover the true function (f(x)) at each (x)?
  • Do intervals widen where data are sparse?
  • Do methods trade off width and coverage sensibly?
  • Are local failures hidden by global averages?

Procedure

The proposed testing process is:

  1. Choose a distribution (P_{X,Y}) for simulating datasets.
  2. Simulate a fixed test set so methods can be compared on the same inputs.
  3. Simulate a training set.
  4. Train the uncertainty estimation method.
  5. Construct prediction intervals and confidence intervals at chosen levels.
  6. Repeat the training-set simulation and model-fitting process many times.
  7. Compute coverage for each test input across simulations.
  8. Compare methods using Brier score and average interval width.

New metrics

The paper introduces pointwise coverage fractions:

  • PICF: Prediction Interval Coverage Fraction, the pointwise coverage of prediction intervals across repeated simulations.
  • CICF: Confidence Interval Coverage Fraction, the pointwise coverage of confidence intervals across repeated simulations.

The authors then use a Brier-style score to penalize deviations from the desired coverage level:

[ BS = _i (PICF(x_i) - (1-))^2 ]

A low Brier score means the intervals are close to the intended coverage level across the input space, not just on average.

Simulation options proposed

The authors suggest three broad ways to build simulations:

  1. Use known toy functions with controlled noise.
  2. Build simulations from real datasets, such as the standard regression benchmark datasets.
  3. Use a very large dataset split into one test set and many distinct training sets.

The third option is practically useful but limited: it can test prediction intervals, but not confidence intervals, because the true function is still unknown.

7. Experiments & Figure-Level Interpretation

Methods compared

The paper uses two methods mainly for demonstration:

  • Naive bootstrap: repeatedly resample the training data, train multiple neural networks, and use variation across the resulting models to construct confidence intervals.
  • Concrete dropout: train a neural network with dropout and keep dropout active during prediction, using repeated stochastic forward passes to estimate uncertainty.

The bootstrap method here is essentially an ensemble method with bootstrap resampling. Its uncertainty estimate comes from the spread across multiple fitted models.

Concrete dropout behaves like a stochastic ensemble at prediction time, but its interval behaviour is not guaranteed to match the intuitive epistemic uncertainty story.

Homoscedastic toy example: Figures 4, 5, and 6

The first toy simulation uses:

[ f(x) = (2x - 1)^3 ]

with constant Gaussian noise.

Figure 4: Both bootstrap and dropout achieve PICP values close to the desired 90% and 80% levels. If the evaluation stopped here, both methods would look acceptable.

Figure 5: When the authors evaluate confidence interval coverage using the true function, the CICP values are much less reassuring. Good PICP does not translate into good confidence interval behaviour.

Figure 6: The authors switch from averaging over test points to averaging over simulations at each (x). This reveals that some input regions are covered too often and others too rarely. This is the key pointwise coverage argument.

Heteroscedastic toy example: Figure 7

The second toy example uses non-constant noise:

[ (x) = 0.1 + x^2 ]

This matters because bootstrap assumes homoscedastic noise in their setup, while concrete dropout can estimate heteroscedastic noise.

Figure 7: PICP can point in the wrong direction. Bootstrap can look comparable or slightly better under global PICP, even though the pointwise evaluation shows worse local interval behaviour. The paper reports a PICF Brier score of 0.011 for bootstrap and 0.0011 for dropout, strongly favouring dropout in this case.

This is one of the clearest demonstrations that average PICP can reward the wrong method.

Sparse-data / out-of-distribution example: Figures 8 and 9

The authors then create a bimodal input distribution so that some regions have limited data.

This is the most relevant part for epistemic uncertainty.

The desired behaviour is:

  • intervals should be narrower where data are dense;
  • intervals should widen where data are sparse;
  • model uncertainty should reflect lack of knowledge about poorly sampled regions.

Figure 8: Bootstrap behaves more sensibly by widening confidence intervals in the sparse region. Dropout does the opposite in the example: its intervals shrink where there is less data.

This is especially important because dropout has better aggregate scores in the example:

  • PICP: dropout 0.83 versus bootstrap 0.75 for 80% intervals;
  • log-likelihood: dropout 0.14 versus bootstrap 0.12.

If the evaluator looked only at PICP or log-likelihood, dropout would appear better. But visually and conceptually, the bootstrap intervals better reflect epistemic uncertainty in the low-data region.

Figure 9: Repeating the experiment 100 times confirms the same concern. Both methods can have reasonable PICP, while their confidence interval behaviour differs substantially in the sparse region.

This figure is useful for the phrasing “a good overall uncertainty score does not guarantee sensible uncertainty behaviour in under-sampled operating regions.”

Boston Housing simulation: Figure 10

To move beyond one-dimensional toy functions, the authors build a simulation from the Boston Housing dataset.

Their procedure:

  1. Train a random forest on the original data and treat it as the true function (f(x)).
  2. Model squared residuals with a second random forest and treat that as (^2(x)).
  3. Simulate new targets using the original covariates and the learned function/noise model.
  4. Evaluate bootstrap and dropout using repeated simulated datasets.

They split the generated data into:

  • 366 training samples;
  • 100 test samples;
  • 40 validation samples.

Figure 10: PICP again looks fairly close to the desired 80% level, but PICF and CICF reveal that individual points are often over-covered or under-covered. The authors report that for PICF, bootstrap has a Brier score of 0.027 and dropout has 0.032. For CICF, bootstrap has 0.147 and dropout has 0.15. Both methods show important local weaknesses.

This section supports the claim that the proposed approach can be made more realistic by using simulations based on real datasets, although it still depends heavily on how the simulator is constructed.

8. My Thoughts, Criticisms & Limitations

Criticism 1: The paper is strongest as a benchmark critique, not a direct deployment recipe

The simulation-based framework is very persuasive for research benchmarking. It lets us test questions that are impossible to answer from ordinary real-world data.

However, in deployed systems we usually do not know (f(x)). This limits direct use of CICP and true pointwise confidence interval coverage.

The practical value is therefore indirect:

  • use simulations to compare methods before deployment;
  • use domain-informed stress tests;
  • use repeated resampling where possible;
  • use local coverage diagnostics on held-out observations;
  • avoid claiming that global PICP proves local reliability.

Criticism 2: Simulation realism becomes the main vulnerability

The paper’s proposed solution depends on the quality of the simulated data-generating process.

If the simulation is too clean, too Gaussian, too low-dimensional, or too unlike the real system, then the evaluation may not generalize. A method can perform well in the benchmark simulator and still fail under real operational conditions.

For energy forecasting, this means the simulation should include meaningful operating regimes such as:

  • seasonal effects;
  • weather regimes;
  • demand levels;
  • forecast horizon;
  • sensor drift;
  • rare high-risk operating states.

Criticism 3: The Gaussian assumption is narrow

Much of the paper assumes normally distributed noise. This is convenient for defining intervals and calculating expectations, but real forecasting errors can be skewed, heavy-tailed, bounded, zero-inflated, multimodal, or regime-dependent.

The authors acknowledge this in the future work section. This matters because quantile regression and conformal methods do not need the same Gaussian assumptions.

Criticism 4: The method comparison is illustrative rather than exhaustive

The paper compares naive bootstrap and concrete dropout mainly because they are easy to implement and useful for exposing metric failures.

It does not provide a broad modern benchmark across:

  • deep ensembles;
  • Bayesian neural networks;
  • Gaussian processes;
  • quantile regression;
  • conformal prediction;
  • evidential methods;
  • natural gradient boosting;
  • distributional tree ensembles.

So the paper should be cited for its evaluation argument, not as final evidence that one uncertainty method dominates another.

Criticism 5: Pointwise coverage is conceptually clean but hard in high dimensions

Pointwise coverage for every exact (x) is attractive theoretically. In high-dimensional real systems, exact repeated observations at the same (x) are rare or impossible.

In practice, the idea may need to become conditional or local coverage by regime, for example:

  • coverage by forecast horizon;
  • coverage by irradiance band;
  • coverage by load level;
  • coverage by season;
  • coverage by temperature range;
  • coverage by confidence/interval-width bins;
  • coverage by learned clusters of operating conditions.

This links the paper closely to Pernot’s adaptivity idea: uncertainty should be reliable across the input space, not only on average.

Criticism 6: Terminology can obscure the applied point

The phrase “single data set” is technically correct but can be misleading for applied readers. The issue is not that a real system is unimportant because it is only one dataset. The issue is that one collected sample from that system may not reveal all uncertainty failures.

A clearer applied phrasing is:

Do not trust global interval coverage unless you have checked whether the intervals behave sensibly across the system’s operating regimes, especially where training data are sparse.

9. Relevance to My Research Context

Why I would cite this paper

I would cite this paper to justify a stricter evaluation standard for probabilistic forecasting models.

It supports the claim that:

  • PICP alone is not enough;
  • log-likelihood alone is not enough;
  • interval width alone is not enough;
  • uncertainty quality must be checked locally or conditionally;
  • sparse regions of the input space require special attention.

How it supports uncertainty-aware forecasting

For load, solar, wind, or electricity-price forecasting, a model can have acceptable average coverage while failing under specific conditions.

Examples:

  • intervals may be too narrow during high ramp events;
  • intervals may be too wide during stable conditions;
  • intervals may fail in rare weather regimes;
  • intervals may be poorly calibrated at longer forecast horizons;
  • intervals may look globally correct because errors cancel across seasons.

This paper gives methodological support for analysing coverage by regime rather than only reporting one global PICP value.

How it connects to explainability

The paper is not an XAI paper, but it is highly relevant to explainable uncertainty.

If I use SHAP or another XAI method to explain interval width, uncertainty, or prediction risk, I should first ask whether those intervals are actually reliable. Explaining a bad uncertainty estimate can produce a very polished but misleading explanation.

The evaluation logic should therefore come before or alongside the explanation logic:

  1. Validate whether the uncertainty estimate behaves sensibly.
  2. Then explain why uncertainty is high or low in particular regions.

Most useful insight for my work

The strongest takeaway is:

A probabilistic model should not only achieve the desired coverage overall; it should express uncertainty correctly in the specific parts of the input space where decisions are made.

This is especially important for operational systems where data density varies across the operating envelope.

10. Clarifications From My Notes

Confidence interval coverage in real life

In real-world data-driven modelling, we usually cannot calculate true confidence interval coverage for (f(x)), because we do not know (f(x)).

If we check whether held-out observed values fall inside intervals, we are checking prediction interval coverage. That is useful, but it is not the same as confidence interval coverage.

Practical alternatives include:

  • repeated train/test splits;
  • cross-validation;
  • blocked or time-series validation;
  • bootstrapping;
  • synthetic simulations;
  • simulator-based stress tests;
  • local coverage diagnostics on observed outcomes.

These can approximate parts of the repeated-experiment logic, but they do not magically reveal the true function.

“Coverage cannot be tested on a single data set”

This means that coverage is a property of the interval-generating procedure over repeated samples and repeated model fits.

One train/test split can estimate how many intervals covered observations in that particular split. It cannot prove that the interval procedure has the claimed coverage generally or locally.

For a real system, this should be read as:

A single sample of system operation may hide uncertainty failures in under-sampled regions of the operating space.

Good PICP but no pointwise coverage

The important idea is cancellation.

Global PICP can look correct when:

  • one region is underconfident and produces intervals that are too wide;
  • another region is overconfident and produces intervals that are too narrow;
  • the average coverage across both regions happens to match the desired value.

This is why conditional coverage by operating regime is more informative than one global coverage number.

Better log-likelihood is not a likelihood test

The paper talks about log-likelihood as a scoring metric, not a traditional likelihood ratio testing procedure.

The key warning is that a better log-likelihood may reflect better point predictions rather than better interval uncertainty.

Bootstrap as an ensemble

The bootstrap method in the paper is naturally understood as an ensemble:

  • resample the data;
  • train multiple models;
  • predict with each model;
  • use the spread across model predictions as an uncertainty estimate.

The “bootstrap” part describes how the ensemble members are generated.

Figure 8 and sparse operating regions

Figure 8 is especially useful because it shows the distinction between a good score and sensible uncertainty behaviour.

Dropout has better aggregate PICP and log-likelihood in that example, but its intervals shrink in the low-data region. Bootstrap has worse aggregate scores, but its intervals widen in the sparse region, which is more consistent with epistemic uncertainty.

This is the paper’s practical warning in one picture.

11. Action Items & Integration

  • Cite this paper when arguing that global PICP is insufficient for validating prediction intervals.
  • Use it to justify reporting local or conditional coverage, not only average coverage.
  • Distinguish carefully between prediction intervals and confidence intervals in my own writing.
  • Do not claim CICP in real data unless the true function is known through simulation or a trusted data-generating model.
  • When discussing sparse regions, avoid vague “one dataset” wording and instead refer to under-sampled operating regimes or limited system coverage.
  • Pair PICP with diagnostics such as coverage by feature regime, forecast horizon, season, demand level, weather state, or interval-width bin.
  • Use simulation-based or surrogate-based stress tests where possible, especially if comparing uncertainty methods.
  • When using XAI to explain uncertainty, first verify that the uncertainty estimates are meaningful in the relevant regimes.
  • Link this paper with Pernot’s calibration, consistency, and adaptivity framework: Sluijterman et al. provide the pointwise-coverage critique; Pernot provides a broader diagnostic vocabulary for local adaptivity.

12. Pre-Written Assets

Literature review paragraph

Sluijterman, Cator, and Heskes argue that common evaluation practices for regression uncertainty estimates can be misleading. In particular, they show that neither test-set log-likelihood nor prediction interval coverage probability is sufficient to establish that uncertainty estimates are locally reliable. Log-likelihood can improve because of better mean prediction rather than better uncertainty estimation, while PICP only measures marginal coverage and can hide overconfident and underconfident regions that cancel on average. The authors therefore propose simulation-based testing in which the data-generating function is known and the modelling process is repeated many times, allowing pointwise prediction and confidence interval coverage to be evaluated through PICF, CICF, Brier scores, and interval width. Their results with bootstrap and dropout methods show that aggregate scores can favour methods with undesirable interval behaviour, especially in sparse regions of the input space.

Methodological caution sentence

Reporting a single PICP value is not enough to establish reliable probabilistic forecasts, because correct average coverage can mask severe local miscalibration across operating regimes.

Practical integration sentence

For operational forecasting, the paper supports evaluating interval coverage conditionally across meaningful regimes such as forecast horizon, weather state, season, demand level, or sparse regions of the input space, rather than relying only on global PICP or log-likelihood.

One-line review

This paper is a methodological warning that uncertainty estimates should be evaluated where they are used, not only averaged into a reassuring global score.

13. Bottom-Line Takeaway

The paper’s main value is its evaluation philosophy.

It does not say that PICP or log-likelihood are useless. It says they answer weaker questions than many researchers assume. PICP answers whether intervals cover observations on average over a test set. Log-likelihood answers how well a predictive density fits held-out data. Neither one proves that uncertainty is correct locally, that model uncertainty is separated from data noise, or that intervals behave sensibly in sparse operating regions.

For my own work, the important lesson is to treat uncertainty validation as a local and regime-aware problem. A probabilistic forecasting model should not merely be calibrated on average; it should be reliable in the parts of the system where the uncertainty will guide decisions.
