UbiQTree: Uncertainty quantification in XAI with tree ensembles

Explainable AI
Uncertainty quantification
SHAP
Tree ensembles
Healthcare AI
Review of a Patterns paper proposing UbiQTree, a framework for decomposing uncertainty in SHAP explanations for tree ensembles.
Published

March 13, 2026

1. Quick Reference & Meta-Data

  • Authors & Year: Akshat Dubey, Aleksandar Anžel, Bahar Ilgen, Georges Hattab (2026)
  • Venue (Journal/Conference): Patterns (high-visibility interdisciplinary journal, especially relevant for applied AI methods)
  • Link/DOI: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.patter.2025.101454
  • Code/Data Available?: Yes, code is available via GitHub and Zenodo
  • Domain/Application Area: Explainable AI, uncertainty quantification, healthcare-oriented ML
  • Proximity/Threat Level: Medium-High / Conceptually important
  • Date Reviewed: 2026-03-13

2. Core Contributions, Novelty & Motivation

  • The Motivation (Why this paper exists):
    • SHAP is widely used to explain tree-based models, especially in healthcare and other high-stakes settings.
    • However, SHAP is typically treated as a point estimate, meaning it tells you what the attribution is but not how stable or trustworthy that attribution is.
    • This is a serious issue in practice because clinicians or decision-makers may over-trust explanations that are actually unstable across plausible model realizations.
  • The Motivation (Why uncertainty in XAI matters):
    • In high-stakes domains, it is not enough for a model to be accurate; the explanation itself must also be reliable.
    • A model may correctly predict a class label while the explanation behind that prediction remains unstable.
    • The paper argues that uncertainty in explanations must be quantified in the same way predictive uncertainty is quantified.
  • Their claim to fame / Novelty:
    • They propose UbiQTree, a framework that performs uncertainty quantification directly on SHAP values for tree ensembles.
    • Their key theoretical move is to decompose SHAP uncertainty into:
      • Aleatoric uncertainty: variability linked to irreducible noise / randomness,
      • Epistemic uncertainty: variability due to incomplete knowledge, limited data, or model uncertainty,
      • Entanglement: covariance between model-related and data-related uncertainty contributions.
    • They combine:
      • Dempster-Shafer theory for belief/plausibility-based uncertainty reasoning,
      • Liu’s uncertainty theory for uncertainty distributions and entropy-based reasoning,
      • Dirichlet process hypothesis sampling to generate plausible sub-ensemble explanations.
  • The paper’s main conceptual insight:
    • The most important features according to mean absolute SHAP are not necessarily the most stable features.
    • So a feature can look globally important while being epistemically fragile.
  • The Value Derived from Explainability (What XAI/UQ actually did here):
    • It moves SHAP from “importance scoring” toward reliability-aware explanation.
    • It allows users to inspect:
      • confidence intervals around SHAP,
      • entropy of SHAP distributions,
      • sign stability,
      • disagreement across sub-models,
      • whether importance and uncertainty are coupled.
  • Their ultimate result:
    • UbiQTree provides a mechanism to audit SHAP reliability rather than just SHAP magnitude.
    • Across several healthcare datasets, the framework shows that some top-ranked features have large epistemic uncertainty and inconsistent directional stability, meaning explanation quality cannot be inferred from feature importance alone.

3. Methodology & Technical Details

  • Base Modeling Setting:
    • The framework is developed around tree ensemble models, particularly bagging-style models such as Random Forests.
    • Their use case validation relies on RF classifiers trained on healthcare datasets.
  • Core Technical Idea: SHAP variance decomposition
    • The paper defines total SHAP variance as the sum of:
      • aleatoric component,
      • epistemic component,
      • minus an entanglement term that captures covariance between data-centric and model-centric expectations.
    • This is important because they argue the usual decomposition can be misleading when model and data uncertainty are not independent.
  • Theoretical Building Blocks:
    • Dempster-Shafer theory (DST):
      • Used to define belief and plausibility over intervals of SHAP values.
      • Useful for representing ambiguity and ignorance rather than only classical probability.
    • Uncertainty theory:
      • Used to define an uncertainty distribution over SHAP values and to reason about entropy and information gain.
    • Dirichlet process (DP) hypothesis sampling:
      • Used to sample plausible weighted sub-ensembles from the trained forest.
      • Trees are weighted using out-of-bag performance.
      • The concentration parameter α controls how concentrated the posterior is around stronger trees versus broader exploration of model variants.
  • Pipeline Summary:
    1. Generate sub-ensembles using Dirichlet-weighted sampling.
    2. Compute TreeSHAP for each sampled sub-ensemble.
    3. Decompose variance into aleatoric, epistemic, and entanglement terms.
    4. Aggregate uncertainty-aware explanation statistics:
      • mean SHAP,
      • standard deviation,
      • 95% credible interval,
      • entropy,
      • sign stability.
  • Uncertainty Metrics They Emphasize:
    • Mean ± 2σ SHAP
    • 95% credible intervals
    • Entropy of SHAP distributions
    • Sign stability
    • Conflict / ambiguity measures from evidence theory
  • Interpretation of their metrics:
    • Wide SHAP distributions imply unstable explanations.
    • Flat or multimodal SHAP densities imply disagreement across sub-models.
    • Low sign stability means the direction of feature effect is not robust.
    • High entropy indicates lower certainty in the explanation.

4. Datasets / Experimental Testbed

The paper validates the framework on several healthcare datasets rather than on a single benchmark. This is a strength because the method is presented as a general uncertainty-aware explanation framework rather than a dataset-specific trick.

Dataset 1: MIMIC-III

  • Task: Length-of-stay classification
  • Setting: Patients grouped into short-stay, medium-stay, and long-stay classes
  • Data Preparation:
    • Removed fields such as hadm_id, religion, marital_status, and ethnicity
    • Numerical features imputed with median and standardized
    • RF classifier with 100 estimators, class balancing, OOB scoring
    • 70:30 train-test split with stratification
  • Reported predictive result: Micro-F1 of 94.70%

Dataset 2: Ovarian Cancer Risk and Progression Dataset

  • Task: Four-class risk classification
  • Class balance: Strongly imbalanced
  • Preprocessing / training:
    • Timestamp and leakage-prone progression variable removed
    • Median imputation, scaling, label encoding
    • SMOTETomek used for rebalancing
    • RF with 20 estimators and max depth 6
  • Reported predictive result: F1 score of 55.7%

Dataset 3: SEER Breast Cancer Dataset

  • Task: Alive vs deceased outcome classification

  • Scale: 4,024 instances, 16 features

  • Context: Longitudinal, clinically relevant survival dataset

  • General experimental design across datasets:

    • Posterior-like sub-model samples are obtained from tree ensembles
    • SHAP is computed repeatedly across these plausible model realizations
    • The resulting distributions are analyzed rather than a single SHAP vector

5. Key Results & What They Mean

MIMIC-III findings

  • For short-stay, medium-stay, and long-stay predictions, the dominant features repeatedly included:
    • NumTransfers
    • NumNotes
    • NumCallouts
  • But the important point is not only that these features rank highly.
  • The framework shows that they also carry substantial epistemic uncertainty.
  • Example:
    • In the short-stay class, NumTransfers, NumNotes, and NumCallouts had epistemic uncertainty values of 0.133, 0.104, and 0.065 respectively.
    • Their sign stabilities were only 79.8%, 77.0%, and 74.5%.
  • So the model relies on these features, but not with fully stable reasoning.

Ovarian cancer findings

  • Top features varied across risk classes, but features such as:
    • SocioeconomicStatus
    • alcohol
    • PreviousTreatment
    • MenstrualHistory
    • symptom showed both importance and substantial variability across model subsets.
  • This reinforces the paper’s main message:
    • explanation magnitude does not imply explanation reliability.

SEER breast cancer findings

  • The paper examines the relationship between mean absolute SHAP and the standard deviation of SHAP values.
  • Their Figure 15 is especially important:
    • a high positive correlation means the model’s “most important” features are also the most unstable.
  • That is a very strong warning for real-world XAI use:
    • users might trust exactly the features they should scrutinize the most.

6. Validation Strategy (Predictions & Explanations)

  • Predictive validation:
    • Standard supervised learning evaluation using held-out test sets and F1 scores.
    • The predictive model is not the main novelty; the explanation analysis is.
  • Explanation validation:
    • Their validation is largely internal and descriptive, not benchmarked against a formal gold standard for explanation correctness.
    • They evaluate explanation reliability using:
      • variance,
      • entropy,
      • credible intervals,
      • sign stability,
      • and visual distributional analysis.
  • What this means:
    • They do not just ask, “Which features matter?”
    • They ask, “How confident should we be that these features matter in the way SHAP says they do?”
  • Operational decision thresholds:
    • The paper even proposes thresholds for interpreting SHAP uncertainty in deployment settings, linking high uncertainty to expert-in-the-loop verification or retraining.

7. Strengths

  • Strength 1: Strong problem framing
    • The paper identifies a real weakness in current SHAP practice: point-valued attributions are often over-interpreted.
    • This is especially persuasive in healthcare.
  • Strength 2: Moves from importance to reliability
    • A major conceptual strength is that it reframes XAI from “feature ranking” to “feature attribution under uncertainty.”
  • Strength 3: Rich uncertainty toolbox
    • The combination of variance decomposition, entropy, sign stability, confidence intervals, and evidence theory gives a genuinely multi-faceted view of explanation quality.
  • Strength 4: Practical with tree ensembles
    • Because it works with bagging-based tree ensembles, it stays in a setting that is still widely used in tabular healthcare ML.
  • Strength 5: Clear practical message
    • The takeaway is simple and important:
      • important does not mean stable.

8. My Thoughts, Criticisms & Unstated Limitations

  • Criticism 1: Heavy theory, lighter empirical proof
    • The paper is theoretically ambitious, but the empirical side is more illustrative than decisive.
    • It demonstrates that the framework produces meaningful diagnostics, but it does not fully prove that these diagnostics improve downstream human decisions or clinical outcomes.
  • Criticism 2: No formal external XAI ground truth
    • They quantify instability very carefully, but they do not establish whether a “stable” explanation is necessarily a “correct” explanation.
    • Stability and correctness are related, but they are not the same thing.
  • Criticism 3: Random Forest-centric validation
    • Although the motivation is stated for tree ensembles broadly, the validation is centered on RF-style bagging.
    • That makes sense because bagging gives natural sub-model variation, but it leaves open how well the same framework transfers to boosting methods such as XGBoost or LightGBM, where tree dependence and sequential fitting complicate the interpretation.
  • Criticism 4: Terminology may overstate aleatoric handling
    • In practice, much of what is being captured here comes from variation across trees, tree subsets, and training-induced instability.
    • That is very naturally epistemic/model-side.
    • The aleatoric interpretation is theoretically motivated, but in the experiments it may be less cleanly separated than the paper’s language suggests.
  • Criticism 5: Computational and conceptual overhead
    • Standard SHAP is attractive partly because it is already understandable to practitioners.
    • UbiQTree adds multiple theoretical layers:
      • DST,
      • uncertainty distributions,
      • Dirichlet processes,
      • variance decomposition,
      • entropy,
      • sign stability.
    • This makes it richer, but also harder to communicate and operationalize outside a research setting.
  • Criticism 6: Thresholds for intervention feel heuristic
    • Their suggested thresholds for expert review, automated decisions, and retraining are useful operationally.
    • But they appear more heuristic than universally validated.
    • In a production setting, these thresholds would need calibration for each domain and risk tolerance.
  • Criticism 7: More comparison to prior SHAP-UQ methods was needed
    • The paper discusses prior work on SHAP uncertainty, but the experimental section would have been stronger if it had directly benchmarked UbiQTree against competing SHAP-UQ methods rather than mostly demonstrating its own outputs.

9. Research Value for My Own Work

  • What I can take from this paper:
    • It gives a very useful conceptual argument that explanation quality should itself be uncertainty-aware.
    • It supports the idea that feature importance rankings alone are incomplete.
    • It provides language and framing for discussing:
      • explanation robustness,
      • attribution stability,
      • uncertainty-aware interpretability.
  • How this could influence my literature review:
    • I can cite this paper as evidence that SHAP-based interpretation in critical systems should not be treated as deterministic.
    • It helps justify the inclusion of uncertainty-aware validation metrics for explainability methods.
  • How this could influence my methodology:
    • If I use SHAP, I should consider reporting:
      • confidence intervals,
      • attribution variance,
      • sign stability,
      • entropy or dispersion metrics, not just average SHAP scores.
  • Positioning insight:
    • This paper is not mainly about improving predictive performance.
    • It is about improving the trustworthiness of explanations.
    • That distinction is important when positioning a contribution.

10. Action Items & Integration

11. Bottom-Line Summary

This paper is important because it attacks a blind spot in modern XAI practice: we often explain models as if the explanations themselves are certain. UbiQTree argues that this is unsafe, especially in healthcare, and proposes a structured framework for quantifying uncertainty in SHAP explanations. Its biggest contribution is not a new predictor, but a new way of thinking about explanation reliability. The most valuable takeaway is simple but powerful: the feature the model talks about most confidently may not be the feature it understands most reliably.
