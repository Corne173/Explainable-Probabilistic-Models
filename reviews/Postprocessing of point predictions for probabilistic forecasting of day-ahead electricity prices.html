Postprocessing of point predictions for probabilistic forecasting of day-ahead electricity prices: The benefits of using isotonic distributional regression

Probabilistic forecasting
Electricity price forecasting
Postprocessing
Isotonic distributional regression
Conformal prediction
Quantile regression averaging
Review of an Energy Economics paper comparing QRA, Conformal Prediction, and Isotonic Distributional Regression for probabilistic day-ahead electricity price forecasting.
Published

March 13, 2026

  • Authors & Year: Arkadiusz Lipiecki, Bartosz Uniejewski, Rafał Weron (2024)
  • Venue (Journal/Conference): Energy Economics
  • DOI: 10.1016/j.eneco.2024.107934
  • Core Topic: Postprocessing point forecasts into full probabilistic forecasts for day-ahead electricity prices
  • Main Methods Studied: Quantile Regression Averaging (QRA), Conformal Prediction (CP), and Isotonic Distributional Regression (IDR)
  • Markets Studied: Germany and Spain
  • Test Horizon: Two 4.5-year out-of-sample periods spanning COVID and the war in Ukraine
  • Code/Data Availability: They mention using public data and an open-source Julia package, PostForecasts.jl, for postprocessing
  • Proximity/Threat Level: High / Directly relevant if I work on probabilistic forecasting, postprocessing, calibration, or distributional evaluation
  • Why this paper matters: It shows that strong probabilistic forecasts do not necessarily require end-to-end distributional deep learning; carefully designed postprocessing on top of strong point forecasts can outperform more complex neural distribution models

2. Core Contributions, Novelty & Motivation

The Motivation (Why probabilistic electricity price forecasting?)

  • The paper starts from a practical market argument: decisions based on predictive distributions of day-ahead electricity prices can yield substantially higher profits than decisions based only on point forecasts.
  • This is especially relevant in electricity markets because prices are volatile, spiky, and regime-dependent, particularly during shocks such as COVID, fuel crises, and the war in Ukraine.
  • In a trading or risk management setting, a point forecast gives only a central guess, while a predictive distribution gives information about tail risk, asymmetry, and forecast confidence.

The Motivation (Why postprocess point forecasts instead of building a probabilistic model from scratch?)

  • The authors argue that most models used in practice and in the literature still generate point predictions only.
  • Rather than discarding that large ecosystem, they propose a practical route: take already strong point forecasts and convert them into probabilistic forecasts through postprocessing.
  • This is attractive because it leverages progress in point forecasting while avoiding the full complexity of bespoke probabilistic model design.

Their claim to fame / novelty

  • This is, according to the authors, the first study to consider Isotonic Distributional Regression (IDR) for electricity price forecasting.
  • It is also one of the first to use Conformal Prediction (CP) in this domain.
  • They do not merely compare methods individually; they also study forecast combination, and use Shapley values to quantify how much each postprocessing component contributes to the final ensemble.
  • Their combined model, LEAR-Ave, beats a state-of-the-art Distributional Deep Neural Network with Johnson SU output (DDNN-JSU) over long and difficult test periods.

The actual scientific value

  • The paper makes a strong case that distributional quality can be improved substantially by intelligent postprocessing, not just by increasingly complex end-to-end deep architectures.
  • It also shows that diversity among probabilistic components matters: the best overall results come not from a single postprocessor, but from combining QRA, CP, and IDR.
  • Shapley analysis reveals that IDR contributes the most to the ensemble, even though its standalone performance is less uniformly stable than QRA.

3. Methodology & Technical Details

Datasets / testbed

  • The authors use publicly available data from:
    • ENTSO-E for day-ahead prices, load forecasts, and renewable generation forecasts
    • Investing.com for carbon emission, natural gas, crude oil, and coal prices
  • Markets:
    • Germany: BZN|DE-LU, historically BZN|DE-AT-LU before 30.09.2018
    • Spain: BZN|ES
  • Time span:
    • 01.01.2015 to 31.12.2023
  • Test period:
    • Starts on 27.06.2019
    • Gives a 4.5-year out-of-sample evaluation
  • Data engineering details:
    • Some ENTSO-E series had 15-minute resolution and were aggregated to hourly
    • DST transitions were explicitly handled by averaging missing/duplicate hours

Point forecasting backbone

The paper is not about inventing a new point forecasting model. Instead, it uses point predictions as inputs to postprocessing.

Main point model: LEAR

  • They use a variant of the LASSO-Estimated AutoRegressive (LEAR) model.
  • Inputs include:
    • lagged electricity prices
    • day-ahead load forecasts
    • day-ahead renewable generation forecasts
    • macro variables: carbon emission prices, natural gas, Brent crude, and coal prices
    • weekday dummies
  • Important implementation choices:
    • They apply an asinh variance-stabilizing transformation to prices
    • They use coordinate descent LASSO
    • They use 7-fold cross-validation
    • They repeat the LEAR estimation 5 times per training window and average the results to reduce randomness

Benchmark point model: naive

  • They also include a simple similar-day naive benchmark
  • Monday, Saturday, and Sunday use the price from the same day last week
  • Other weekdays use yesterday’s price

Forecast generation setup

  • Rolling window estimation
  • Forecasts are generated daily for all 24 hours of the next day
  • LEAR uses four training window lengths:
    • 56, 84, 1092, and 1456 days
  • Postprocessing is trained separately by hour using calibration windows:
    • 28, 56, 91, and 182 days

4. Postprocessing Methods Compared

4.1 Quantile Regression Averaging (QRA / QRM variant)

  • QRA estimates quantiles directly using quantile regression.
  • The authors test several variants and retain the best-performing one:
    • a Quantile Regression Machine (QRM)-style formulation using the average point forecast as the regressor
  • Strengths:
    • Direct quantile estimation
    • Flexible and well-established in energy forecasting
    • Generally strong and stable overall performer
  • Weaknesses:
    • Computationally the heaviest of the postprocessors in the paper because a separate optimization is needed for each quantile

4.2 Conformal Prediction (CP)

  • CP constructs prediction intervals using recent absolute forecast errors.
  • It is attractive because it provides valid intervals under weak assumptions and requires no parametric distributional assumption.
  • In this paper, CP is centered around the average point forecast.
  • Limitation:
    • Quantiles are derived under an implicit symmetry assumption around the point forecast
    • This can be restrictive in electricity prices, where distributions are often asymmetric and heavy-tailed

4.3 Isotonic Distributional Regression (IDR)

  • IDR is the most novel method here.
  • It is a nonparametric distributional learning method under a stochastic order / isotonicity constraint.
  • Intuition:
    • If one point forecast is higher than another, the corresponding predicted distribution should shift in an orderly way
  • Why it fits this problem:
    • Point forecasts are natural ordering variables for subsequent conditional distributions
  • Implementation:
    • The authors fit separate IDR distributions for each of the four point forecast models
    • Then they combine them by vertical averaging
  • Key property:
    • IDR directly minimizes CRPS under isotonic constraints
  • Important empirical finding:
    • IDR is the most behaviorally varied of the three methods
    • That variation appears to be exactly why it adds the most value in the ensemble

4.4 Ensemble of predictive distributions: LEAR-Ave

  • Final ensemble = average of:
    • LEAR-QRM
    • LEAR-CP
    • LEAR-IDR
  • The paper’s strongest message is that the combination beats all standalone methods, including the sophisticated DDNN benchmark

4.5 Additional probabilistic benchmarks

They also compare against simpler Gaussian-error-based probabilistic benchmarks: - Naive-1N - Naive-N - LEAR-N

These assume prediction errors are normally distributed with estimated standard deviation.

4.6 Distributional deep learning benchmark

  • The main modern benchmark is DDNN-JSU
  • It is a Distributional Deep Neural Network outputting the parameters of a Johnson SU distribution
  • This is the “complex neural competitor” that the postprocessing approaches are tested against

5. Validation Strategy

Forecast evaluation

The paper is strong in evaluation design.

Test setting

  • Two large markets
  • 4.5-year test periods
  • Includes highly nonstationary and crisis-heavy conditions

Main metric

  • CRPS (Continuous Ranked Probability Score)
  • This is the central metric for probabilistic forecast quality

Secondary analysis

  • APS20 for the extreme 20 percentiles, targeting tail/risk performance
  • Conditional Predictive Ability (CPA) test to assess whether forecast differences are statistically significant
  • Rolling 182-day skill scores to inspect temporal stability and regime sensitivity
  • Shapley values to assess contribution of each ensemble component

Why this validation is strong

  • They do not stop at average performance
  • They check:
    • overall distribution quality
    • temporal robustness
    • tail performance
    • statistical significance
    • component contribution in the ensemble

That is a real strength of the paper.

6. Main Results

Overall CRPS performance

  • LEAR-Ave is the best model overall across both Germany and Spain and across all four subperiods.
  • The simple Gaussian and naive baselines perform worst, as expected.
  • The LEAR-based postprocessing family consistently outperforms the deep distributional benchmark in most settings.

The most important headline result

  • The combined postprocessed forecast outperforms DDNN-JSU, despite the latter being a much more complex model.

Relative behavior of the three postprocessors

  • QRA / LEAR-QRM:
    • Strong all-rounder
    • Stable
    • Often the best standalone method
  • CP / LEAR-CP:
    • Stable but usually the weakest contributor of the three
    • Useful in the ensemble, but not the star
  • IDR / LEAR-IDR:
    • Most uneven over time
    • Sometimes weaker in volatile transition periods
    • But in certain regimes, especially calmer periods after volatile ones, it becomes very strong
    • Contributes the most to the ensemble according to Shapley values

Tail-risk results

  • In the tails, the story becomes more nuanced.
  • LEAR-Ave still performs best overall across the full test sets.
  • However, DDNN-JSU shows strength in some tail-focused settings, especially right after hyperparameter optimization.
  • The authors therefore do not dismiss DDNN-JSU entirely; instead, they argue it may have tail-performance potential, but at very high tuning cost.

Computational findings

  • Postprocessing is far cheaper than frequent deep model hyperparameter tuning.
  • Approximate timing reported:
    • LEAR: around 2.5 to 3 hours
    • QRA: 10 to 15 minutes
    • CP: 10 to 15 seconds
    • IDR: 15 to 20 seconds
    • LEAR-Ave: around 2.75 to 3.25 hours total
    • DDNN-JSU: around 6 to 6.5 hours for forecasting, while hyperparameter optimization could take weeks
  • This makes the postprocessing route extremely attractive in practice

7. What Shapley Analysis Revealed

One of the most interesting parts of the paper is that Shapley values are not used for feature importance, but for ensemble-component importance.

What they found

  • Across both Germany and Spain, IDR contributes the most to LEAR-Ave
  • CP contributes the least
  • In 2023, IDR’s contribution exceeds 75% in both markets

Why this matters

  • IDR is not necessarily the most stable standalone model
  • But it brings the most diversity and the most marginal value when combined with QRA and CP
  • This is a really important lesson: the most valuable component in an ensemble is not always the one with the most stable standalone average score

8. My Thoughts, Criticisms & Unstated Limitations

Criticism 1: Strong paper, but still heavily tied to a strong point forecasting backbone

  • The postprocessing works well partly because the base point forecasts are already strong.
  • That means some of the gains may reflect the strength of the improved LEAR implementation, not only the postprocessing scheme itself.
  • In other words, this is not a “pure postprocessing comparison in isolation”; it is a postprocessing comparison on top of a carefully optimized point forecasting pipeline.

Criticism 2: IDR’s success is real, but also regime-dependent

  • The authors are honest that IDR is the most uneven method over time.
  • That means IDR may not be a universal winner.
  • Its strongest value seems to come from complementing other methods, not necessarily replacing them.

Criticism 3: CP is somewhat handicapped by symmetry

  • Conformal Prediction is attractive for validity, but in this setup it is transformed into quantiles via a symmetry-centered construction.
  • Electricity price distributions are often not symmetric, especially during crisis periods and spikes.
  • So the paper’s CP implementation may understate how competitive more advanced conformalized or asymmetric conformal methods could be.

Criticism 4: Benchmark fairness versus DDNN-JSU

  • The paper compares against DDNN-JSU using a fixed hyperparameter set optimized earlier.
  • That is practical and defensible, but it does mean the deep benchmark may be under-optimized relative to what is theoretically possible.
  • The authors acknowledge this themselves.
  • So the paper proves that postprocessing can beat realistically deployed deep distributional models, but not necessarily the best imaginable re-tuned version of them.

Criticism 5: Distributional assumptions are not equally stressed

  • The Gaussian baselines are obviously simplistic and mostly serve as weak references.
  • That is fine, but it means the main real contest is QRA vs CP vs IDR vs DDNN-JSU.
  • I would have liked to see stronger modern alternatives too, for example:
    • direct quantile neural networks
    • conformalized quantile regression
    • mixture density models
    • nonparametric distributional learners beyond isotonicity

Criticism 6: Interpretability is only lightly touched

  • Shapley values here are used to interpret ensemble contribution, not the economics or drivers of price formation.
  • That is useful, but different from explaining the market itself.
  • So this is not really an XAI paper in the broad electricity-price sense; it is more a paper about performance attribution inside an ensemble.

Criticism 7: Heavy dependence on calibration design

  • The success of the method depends on:
    • window lengths
    • rolling recalibration
    • averaging scheme
    • market regime
  • Those are practical strengths, but also tuning levers.
  • This raises a portability question: how much of the reported advantage would remain under different calibration schedules or other markets outside Germany and Spain?

9. What This Paper Does Especially Well

  • It is practical, not just methodological
  • It evaluates over long and difficult market periods
  • It uses statistical significance testing, not only metric ranking
  • It studies tails separately
  • It includes computational cost
  • It uses Shapley values cleverly to explain ensemble value
  • It demonstrates that simple + well-calibrated + diverse can beat complex + expensive

This is one of the paper’s biggest lessons.

10. Positioning Relative to My Own Work

This paper is highly relevant if I am working on: - probabilistic forecasting - calibration/postprocessing of point forecasts - ensemble distributions - uncertainty quantification in energy systems - risk-sensitive forecasting

What I can borrow

  • The idea of treating probabilistic forecasting as a postprocessing problem
  • The use of CRPS + tail metrics + CPA tests
  • The notion that diversity among probabilistic components matters
  • Shapley-value attribution at the ensemble component level

What I can improve on

  • Add stronger modern probabilistic baselines
  • Explore asymmetric or adaptive conformal methods
  • Test whether IDR-style gains persist in:
    • other power systems
    • renewable forecasting
    • load forecasting
    • highly nonstationary non-price time series
  • Separate the effect of:
    • base point model quality
    • postprocessor quality
    • ensemble diversity

11. Action Items & Integration

12. Final Takeaway

This paper is a strong argument for postprocessing as a serious probabilistic forecasting strategy, not just a lightweight add-on. Its central message is not merely that IDR works, but that a diverse ensemble of simple, well-designed postprocessors on top of strong point forecasts can outperform more complicated end-to-end distributional deep learning systems.

If I had to summarize the paper in one line:

Do not assume the best probabilistic forecast must come from the most complex probabilistic model; in hard electricity markets, smart postprocessing and forecast combination can win.
