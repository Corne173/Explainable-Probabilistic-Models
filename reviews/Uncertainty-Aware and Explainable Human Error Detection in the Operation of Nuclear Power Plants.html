Uncertainty-Aware and Explainable Human Error Detection in the Operation of Nuclear Power Plants

Uncertainty quantification
Explainable AI
Nuclear power plants
Human error detection
Bayesian neural networks
Review of a Nuclear Technology paper integrating Bayesian uncertainty quantification and SHAP explanations for multiclass human error detection in nuclear power plant operation.
Published

July 23, 2024

  • Authors & Year: Bhavya Reddy, Ezgi Gursel, Katy Daniels, Anahita Khojandi, Jamie Baalis Coble, Vivek Agarwal, Ronald Boring, Vaibhav Yadav, and Mahboubeh Madadi (2024)
  • Venue (Journal/Conference): Nuclear Technology
  • DOI: 10.1080/00295450.2024.2372217
  • Published Online: 23 July 2024
  • Core Domain: Human error detection in nuclear power plant operations
  • Main Technical Themes: Multiclass classification, uncertainty quantification (UQ), explainable AI (XAI), Bayesian neural networks (BNNs), SHAP
  • Code/Data Available?: Not explicitly provided in the paper extract I reviewed
  • Proximity/Threat Level: Moderate to High for anyone working on trustworthy AI in safety-critical monitoring
  • Date Reviewed: 2026-03-13

2. Core Contributions, Novelty & Motivation

  • The Motivation (Why this matters):
    • In nuclear power plants, operators must monitor many variables in short time windows and identify incidents rapidly.
    • This cognitive burden can itself increase the chance of human error.
    • If an operator incorrectly identifies an abnormal situation, the resulting mitigation action may also be wrong, which is dangerous in a safety-critical environment.
  • Why AI alone is not enough:
    • A classifier that merely predicts an error class is not sufficient in this domain.
    • Operators and supervisors also need to know whether the prediction is trustworthy.
    • In other words, the system must not only be accurate, but also transparent about its confidence.
  • Why Explainability is important here:
    • XAI is used to answer why a prediction was made.
    • In this paper, that means identifying which probe-location and vibration-derived features drove the model toward a given error class.
    • This is especially useful in operator-support settings, where a prediction should ideally be translated into actionable feedback.
  • Why Uncertainty Quantification is important here:
    • Safety-critical systems require more than point predictions.
    • The model should indicate when it is unsure, especially when data are limited and errors can be costly.
    • The paper explicitly distinguishes between aleatory uncertainty (inherent variability in data) and epistemic uncertainty (model uncertainty due to limited knowledge or limited data).
  • Their claim to fame / Novelty:
    • The paper positions itself as addressing a research gap in the joint use of XAI and UQ, specifically for human error classification in nuclear power plants.
    • Rather than treating explainability and uncertainty as separate post-processing ideas, the authors attempt to integrate them into one workflow.
    • Their emphasis is not just on explaining predictions, but also on explaining how certain the model is about those predictions.
  • What they actually contribute:
    • They compare multiple multiclass classifiers, including custom uncertainty-aware neural networks, custom XGBoost variants, ensemble combinations, and Bayesian neural networks.
    • They select the best-performing model not just by classification metrics, but also by UQ behavior.
    • They then use SHAP to analyze both:
      1. which features drive predictions, and
      2. which features appear linked to predictive uncertainty.
  • Their ultimate result:
    • The Bayesian neural network (BNN) was the strongest overall model.
    • It achieved the best balance of predictive performance and uncertainty behavior, outperforming the custom and ensemble alternatives on most metrics.
    • The most challenging classes were Error B and Error D, and these became the main focus of the explainability analysis.

3. Methodology & Technical Details

  • Problem Formulation:
    • Multiclass classification of operator measurement behavior into:
      • No error
      • Error A: Off-axis probe orientation
      • Error B: Off-position first measurement
      • Error C: Off-position second measurement
      • Error D: Swapped locations
  • Data Source / Testbed:
    • The dataset came from a physical flow loop test bed at the Department of Nuclear Engineering, University of Tennessee, Knoxville.
    • Surveillance data were manually collected using Fluke handheld probes.
    • Tri-axial acceleration data and pump speed streams were used.
  • Dataset Size:
    • Total instances: 85
      • 45 normal
      • 10 instances each for four error types
    • This is a very small dataset for a five-class classification problem.
  • Train/Test/Validation Split:
    • 70% train
    • 25% test
    • 5% validation
    • In count terms:
      • Train: 50
      • Test: 24
      • Validation: 11
  • Sampling / Signal Characteristics:
    • Accelerometer data were collected at 25.6 kHz
    • 60 000 samples were collected roughly every 5 minutes
    • Each recording covered about 2.3 seconds
  • Preprocessing Challenges:
    • The raw Fluke outputs were originally in PDF graph form
    • Data had to be manually extracted using Engauge Digitizer
    • This means the dataset is not purely sensor-native in the ML sense; it carries a manual digitization bottleneck
    • That introduces potential granularity loss and human preprocessing bias
  • Feature Construction / Signal Handling:
    • Frequency values were bucketed to identify informative acceleration bands
    • The retained bands were:
      • Axial: 1827 to 2327 Hz
      • Tangential: 41 to 541 Hz
      • Radial: 2249 to 2750 Hz
    • FFT was used to move into the frequency domain
    • The frequencies were then merged with amplitudes for the selected bands
  • Validation Strategy:
    • Because of the small sample size, the authors used 10-fold cross-validation
    • Hyperparameters were tuned using grid search constrained by the validation set

4. Models Compared

The paper compares several uncertainty-aware and ensemble-style models.

4.1 Neural Network with Custom Loss

  • A multilayer perceptron was trained with a custom loss:
    • Cross-entropy loss
    • plus an entropy term
  • The goal was to optimize not only correctness, but also confidence behavior.
  • A scalar lambda controlled the tradeoff between classification fit and entropy regularization.

4.2 XGBoost with Custom Loss

  • XGBoost was modified to include an entropy component in its objective.
  • This was meant to make the model uncertainty-aware while retaining the strong predictive behavior of boosted trees.

4.3 Ensemble Models

The paper constructed ensemble combinations motivated by: - XGBoost having the best traditional accuracy - Neural networks being comparatively more “certain” under the custom loss setup

The ensemble variants included combinations such as: - Traditional NN + custom XGBoost - Custom NN + XGBoost - Custom NN + custom XGBoost - Traditional XGBoost + simple neural network

4.4 Bayesian Neural Network (BNN)

  • The BNN is the paper’s most important model.
  • It uses:
    • Cross-entropy loss
    • plus KL divergence
  • This introduces a probabilistic treatment of weights and allows epistemic uncertainty to be modeled directly.
  • This is particularly suitable for:
    • safety-critical settings
    • limited data regimes
    • situations where calibrated confidence matters

5. Explainability & Uncertainty Framework

  • Why SHAP was chosen:
    • The authors justify SHAP on the basis of:
      • cooperative game theory foundation
      • local accuracy
      • broad applicability across model families
      • ability to capture feature interactions
    • They explicitly note that gradient-based explainers would not transfer as well across all models used in the study.
  • How UQ was integrated:
    • UQ was not treated as a separate calibration add-on only.
    • Instead, the authors used UQ measures to understand:
      • prediction certainty
      • the relationship between accuracy and confidence
      • how feature contributions vary when predictions are certain vs uncertain
  • What this means conceptually:
    • The study’s main conceptual move is:
      • do not just explain what the model predicted
      • also explain how trustworthy that prediction seems to be

6. Evaluation Strategy

6.1 Performance Metrics

The authors used metrics suitable for imbalanced multiclass classification: - Accuracy - Weighted F1 score - AUC-PR - Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC)

6.2 Uncertainty Metrics

They also used multiple UQ-oriented metrics: - Average entropy - Multiclass Brier score - Mutual information (MI) for epistemic uncertainty - Difference between the top two predicted probabilities

6.3 Why this is a good evaluation choice

  • This is one of the stronger parts of the paper.
  • They do not rely on accuracy alone.
  • They acknowledge that in safety-critical classification, a model that is accurate but poorly calibrated or overconfident can still be dangerous.

7. Key Results

7.1 Lambda Sensitivity

  • For the custom-loss neural network and custom-loss XGBoost, lambda was varied between 0.1 and 0.9
  • As lambda increased:
    • entropy increased
    • confidence behavior changed
    • predictive metrics could improve or degrade depending on whether the model moved away from overconfidence or toward excessive uncertainty
  • Observed trend:
    • Higher lambda led to greater entropy, as expected
    • Weighted F1 gradually decreased
    • Brier score worsened at higher lambda values
  • This suggests that too much emphasis on entropy regularization can make the model overly uncertain and less effective

7.2 Comparative Model Performance

  • The BNN emerged as the strongest overall model.
  • Reported metrics for the BNN:
    • Accuracy: 0.95 ± 0.72
    • AUC-PR: 0.81 ± 0.17
    • Weighted F1: 0.95 ± 0.08
    • MCC: 0.92 ± 0.13
    • Brier score: 0.07 ± 0.12
    • MI: 1.04 ± 0.36
    • Average entropy: 0.07 ± 0.06
  • Interpretation:
    • The BNN had the best overall balance of:
      • discrimination
      • calibration
      • confidence quality
      • classwise balance
  • Interesting nuance:
    • Some ensemble models achieved stronger AUC-PR than the BNN
    • But the BNN was still preferred because its overall behavior across both predictive and uncertainty metrics was more balanced and robust

8. Uncertainty Insights

  • The paper provides a classwise uncertainty analysis using repeated BNN passes.

8.1 Class-Level Probability Histograms

  • The BNN was run 100 times per sample
  • The resulting probability distributions were plotted classwise

8.2 What the histograms showed

  • No-error class: highly concentrated near probabilities between 0.97 and 1
    • Very confident and stable predictions
  • Error A: similarly concentrated near 1
    • Also highly certain
  • Error B: much more spread out
    • Some predictions still peaked near 1
    • Others spread roughly from 0.4 to 0.88
    • This indicates substantial variation in certainty
  • Error C: mostly confident, but with some moderately uncertain points
  • Error D: also showed mixed certainty
    • Some points clustered high
    • Others spread between 0.40 and 0.76

8.3 Interpretation

  • The model handles No Error and Error A well
  • The main epistemic difficulty appears around Error B and Error D
  • This is exactly the kind of behavior that makes uncertainty-aware analysis valuable:
    • the model is effectively signaling where it is less trustworthy

8.4 Top-Two Probability Gap Analysis

  • The paper also examined the difference between the top two class probabilities

  • Large differences imply stronger decisiveness

  • Small differences imply uncertainty

  • Observed pattern:

    • No Error and Error A mostly sat in the “accurate and certain” region
    • Error B often showed high accuracy but lower certainty
    • Error D had lower decisiveness and lower accuracy more often

  • That distinction is important:

    • Error B may be recoverably ambiguous
    • Error D may be structurally harder for the model

9. Explainability Insights

This is the most interesting section conceptually.

9.1 What they try to explain

The authors do not stop at “feature importance.” They specifically look at feature contributions under three scenarios: 1. Accurate and Certain 2. Accurate and Uncertain 3. Inaccurate and Certain

That is a very useful framing because it separates: - reliable decisions - cautious-but-correct decisions - confidently wrong decisions

9.2 Main classes analyzed

  • Error B
  • Error D

These were chosen because they were the most uncertainty-relevant classes.

9.3 Error B findings

  • For an accurate and certain Error B prediction:
    • surv_loc_one_tangential had a strong positive contribution
    • surv_loc_two_tangential contributed negatively
  • This aligns well with the physical definition of Error B:
    • the mistake is associated with the first measurement location
  • For accurate but uncertain Error B cases:
    • features such as:
      • surv_loc_two_tangential
      • surv_loc_one_radial.4
      • surv_loc_two_tangential.4
      • surv_loc_two_axial.4 appeared influential
  • These were moderate rather than decisive, suggesting the model sensed the correct class but without full confidence

9.4 Error D findings

  • For accurate and certain Error D cases:
    • surv_loc_one_axial contributed positively
    • features such as:
      • surv_loc_one_radial
      • surv_loc_one_tangential.1
      • surv_loc_two_tangential.4 interacted in a more balanced way
  • This makes intuitive sense because Error D involves swapped locations, so both measurement sites matter

9.5 High-variance SHAP features

  • The paper also discusses SHAP variance at a more global level
  • Features with high SHAP variance were interpreted as possible contributors to predictive uncertainty
  • The authors note that features contributing to uncertainty and inaccuracy in pointwise SHAP analysis also tended to have high variance globally

9.6 Why this is useful

  • This is not merely descriptive XAI
  • It opens the door to operator-facing decision support
  • A future system could potentially say things like:
    • “The issue likely involves probe placement at location 1”
    • “The model is not fully certain, so supervisor verification is advised”

That is far more useful than a raw class label alone.

10. Strengths of the Paper

  • Strong safety-critical framing
    • The paper understands that trust, calibration, and confidence matter in nuclear settings.
  • Joint treatment of XAI and UQ
    • This is the paper’s biggest conceptual strength.
    • Many papers do either feature attribution or uncertainty, but not both in a unified way.
  • Good use of BNNs for small-data uncertainty-aware classification
    • The dataset is very small, so a probabilistic model class is a reasonable choice.
  • Thoughtful metric selection
    • The inclusion of MCC, AUC-PR, Brier score, entropy, MI, and top-two gap is better than a shallow accuracy-only comparison.
  • Operator-oriented interpretation
    • The authors are clearly thinking about practical deployment, not just classifier benchmarking.
  • Useful analysis of “accurate but uncertain” vs “inaccurate but certain”
    • That distinction is extremely valuable in trustworthy AI.

11. My Thoughts, Criticisms & Unstated Limitations

Criticism 1: Very small dataset

  • The dataset has only 85 instances total, with only 10 samples per error class.
  • That is extremely small for a five-class classification problem, especially one involving neural and Bayesian models.
  • Even with 10-fold cross-validation, instability is likely.
  • Some of the reported standard deviations also suggest variability.

Criticism 2: Manual digitization bottleneck

  • A major practical limitation is that data were manually extracted from PDF graphs using Engauge Digitizer.
  • That creates a preprocessing bottleneck and may introduce:
    • discretization error
    • extraction inconsistency
    • human preprocessing bias
  • This reduces confidence that the pipeline would scale cleanly to real plant settings.

Criticism 3: Explainability validation is still mostly qualitative

  • The SHAP analysis is interesting and plausible, but it is still largely a domain-consistency argument
  • The paper does not appear to use formal XAI quality metrics such as:
    • fidelity
    • stability
    • robustness under perturbation
    • explanation consistency across folds
  • So the explanations are persuasive, but not rigorously validated

Criticism 4: “Uncertainty-aware” does not automatically mean “well-calibrated”

  • The paper uses several uncertainty metrics, which is good
  • But there is still limited discussion of formal calibration diagnostics beyond entropy and Brier score
  • For a deployment paper, one might also want:
    • reliability diagrams
    • expected calibration error
    • risk-coverage analysis
    • abstention analysis

Criticism 5: Weak generalization evidence

  • The data come from a single experimental test bed
  • The paper does not establish how well the method would transfer across:
    • other hardware
    • other probe operators
    • other surveillance tasks
    • real plant conditions rather than controlled experimental conditions

Criticism 6: Limited comparator set

  • The comparison is useful, but still somewhat narrow
  • There is no comparison against stronger modern uncertainty baselines such as:
    • deep ensembles
    • Monte Carlo dropout
    • evidential classifiers
    • temperature-scaled deterministic networks
    • calibrated gradient boosting baselines
  • So while the BNN wins in this study, it is not necessarily proven superior to the broader uncertainty-aware literature

Criticism 7: Some feature names are difficult to interpret operationally

  • The SHAP outputs are technically informative but not yet operator-friendly
  • The authors acknowledge this and propose translating features into operator-understandable outputs in future work
  • That is a key step that is still missing for real usability

12. Research Value for My Own Work

  • What makes this paper useful:
    • It is a strong example of combining predictive modeling, uncertainty analysis, and explanation in a safety-critical context
    • It gives a practical template for analyzing:
      • accurate/certain
      • accurate/uncertain
      • inaccurate/certain cases
    • That framing is highly reusable in other trustworthy-AI papers
  • What I would borrow conceptually:
    • Joint XAI + UQ framing
    • Separating classwise uncertainty behavior
    • Using SHAP not only to explain predictions, but also to inspect uncertainty-driving features
    • Highlighting “confidently wrong” predictions as a risk category
  • What I would improve if I built on this:
    • Use a larger and more realistic dataset
    • Add formal calibration evaluation
    • Add more rigorous XAI validation
    • Benchmark against stronger UQ baselines
    • Translate feature explanations into task-level, human-readable alerts

13. Action Items & Integration

14. Overall Takeaway

This paper is not just about classifying human error in nuclear operations. Its real contribution is showing how uncertainty quantification and explainability can be combined to build a more trustworthy classifier in a safety-critical setting. The Bayesian neural network gave the strongest overall performance, and the SHAP analysis helped reveal why difficult classes like Error B and Error D remained uncertain.

The paper is strongest as a trustworthy-AI workflow paper rather than as a definitive benchmark paper. Its main weaknesses come from the tiny dataset, limited comparator set, and mainly qualitative explanation validation. Still, it is a genuinely useful contribution because it moves beyond the shallow “accurate black box” mindset and asks the far more important question: when the model makes a decision, how much should an operator trust it, and why?
