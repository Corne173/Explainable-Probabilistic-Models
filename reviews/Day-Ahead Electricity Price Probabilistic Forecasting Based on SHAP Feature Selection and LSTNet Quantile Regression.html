Day-Ahead Electricity Price Probabilistic Forecasting Based on SHAP Feature Selection and LSTNet Quantile Regression

Review of an Energies paper combining SHAP-based feature selection with LSTNet quantile regression and KDE for probabilistic day-ahead electricity price forecasting.
Published

March 13, 2026

1. Quick Reference & Meta-Data

  • Authors & Year: Huixin Liu, Xiaodong Shen, Xisheng Tang, Junyong Liu (2023)
  • Venue (Journal/Conference): Energies
  • DOI: 10.3390/en16135152
  • Main Topic: Probabilistic day-ahead electricity price forecasting
  • Core Methods: SHAP feature selection, LSTNet quantile regression, kernel density estimation (KDE)
  • Dataset / Market: Danish electricity market, forecasting the DK1 day-ahead tariff
  • Time Span of Data: 1 May 2019 to 31 August 2019
  • Train/Test Split: Training: 1 May 2019 to 31 July 2019; Testing: 1 August 2019 to 31 August 2019
  • Primary Contribution Type: Hybrid forecasting framework combining interpretability, feature selection, and probabilistic forecasting
  • Proximity / Threat Level: Moderate to High, especially for research involving SHAP-based feature selection in energy-market forecasting

2. Core Contributions, Novelty & Motivation

  • The Motivation (Why electricity price forecasting matters):
    • Electricity prices are central to electricity-market decision making and directly influence resource allocation, bidding, and operational planning.
    • Rising renewable penetration has increased volatility and uncertainty, making price behavior harder to model accurately.
    • The authors argue that deterministic forecasting alone is insufficient because it produces only a single point estimate and does not quantify uncertainty.
  • The Motivation (Why probabilistic forecasting):
    • Electricity prices in modern power systems are noisy, volatile, and influenced by many interacting drivers.
    • Probabilistic forecasting provides richer information than point forecasts by estimating quantiles, intervals, and density functions.
    • This is especially useful for market participants making risk-sensitive decisions under uncertainty.
  • The Motivation (Why SHAP / interpretability):
    • The input feature space in integrated electricity markets can become large and redundant.
    • The authors use SHAP not only as an interpretation tool, but also as a feature selection mechanism.
    • Their framing is that SHAP helps reveal both which variables matter most and how they influence the final forecast, including directionality.
  • Their claim to novelty:
    • They propose a new probabilistic electricity price forecasting framework that combines:
      1. SHAP-based feature selection,
      2. LSTNet quantile regression,
      3. KDE-based recovery of probability density functions and prediction intervals.
    • Their novelty is less about inventing a brand-new model family and more about integrating interpretable feature selection with nonparametric probabilistic forecasting in a structured pipeline.
  • What the paper is really contributing:
    • A feature-selection stage using SHAP values to reduce redundancy and overfitting.
    • A probabilistic forecasting stage where LSTNet predicts multiple quantiles.
    • A nonparametric density reconstruction step using Gaussian kernel density estimation.
    • Qualitative market interpretation through SHAP feature-importance and dependence analysis.

3. Methodology & Technical Details

3.1 Forecasting Philosophy

  • The paper positions itself explicitly in the nonparametric probabilistic forecasting camp.
  • The authors criticize parametric probabilistic methods because real power-system outputs can be highly stochastic, fat-tailed, and polymorphic, making rigid parametric assumptions unreliable.
  • Their workaround is:
    • estimate quantiles directly via quantile regression,
    • then reconstruct the implied probability density using KDE.

3.2 SHAP for Feature Selection and Analysis

  • SHAP is used in two distinct ways:
    1. Feature selection: rank variables by average absolute SHAP value,
    2. Feature analysis: inspect whether features increase or decrease price and how this depends on feature magnitude.
  • The feature importance score is computed as the average absolute SHAP contribution for a feature across samples.
  • Unlike plain correlation-based ranking, SHAP preserves both:
    • magnitude of influence, and
    • direction of influence.

3.3 LSTNet Architecture

  • The forecasting backbone is LSTNet, chosen because electricity prices exhibit:
    • short-term local dependencies,
    • long-term cyclical dependencies,
    • nonlinear behavior.
  • LSTNet contains:
    • a CNN module for short-term local feature extraction,
    • a recurrent / recurrent-skip module for long-term temporal dependence,
    • an autoregressive linear module to restore sensitivity to scale changes.
  • This last point is important: the authors explicitly note that the nonlinear components can become insensitive to input scale, so the AR branch is added to correct that weakness.

3.4 Quantile Regression Layer

  • The paper explains quantile regression from the ground up:
    • standard linear quantile regression,
    • neural-network quantile regression,
    • then LSTNet quantile regression.
  • In practice, the model predicts a set of electricity-price quantiles rather than a single expected value.
  • The paper states that the inter-quantile interval is 0.1, so they appear to forecast quantiles on a coarse grid such as 0.1, 0.2, …, 0.9.

3.5 Density Reconstruction with KDE

  • After obtaining predicted quantiles, the authors use Gaussian kernel density estimation to reconstruct a continuous predictive distribution.
  • Bandwidth is chosen via the Rule-of-Thumb formula.
  • This step lets them move from a discrete set of predicted quantiles to:
    • a probability density function,
    • and prediction intervals at different confidence levels.

3.6 Evaluation Metrics

  • Point forecasting metrics: RMSE and MAPE
  • Interval / probabilistic metrics:
    • PICP: Prediction Interval Coverage Probability
    • PINAW: Prediction Interval Average Width
    • AL: Average pinball loss
  • The paper emphasizes the trade-off between:
    • coverage / reliability and
    • sharpness / narrow intervals
  • They use average pinball loss as the tie-breaker when PICP and PINAW point in opposite directions.

4. Dataset / Testbed

  • Market: Danish electricity market
  • Target: DK1 day-ahead tariff
  • Data source: www.energidataservice.dk
  • Time span: 1 May 2019 to 31 August 2019
  • Training period: 1 May 2019 to 31 July 2019
  • Testing period: 1 August 2019 to 31 August 2019

Input Features

The paper includes lagged regional electricity prices and market/renewable/exchange variables such as: - DK1, DK2 lagged prices, - neighboring market prices: DE, NO2, SE3, SE4, SYS, - DK1 production and consumption, - wind, solar, and hydropower, - exchange variables between DK1 and neighboring regions/countries.

Important practical note

  • This is a very short study period for electricity price forecasting: only about four months in total, with roughly three months used for training.
  • That makes the paper more of a method demonstration than a strong stress-test across seasons, years, structural breaks, and market regime shifts.

5. Feature Selection Findings & Interpretability Results

5.1 Top-ranked Features

The top SHAP-ranked inputs are: 1. DK2_7 2. DK1_7 3. DK1_1 4. DE_1 5. DK2_1 6. ExGE_1 7. HydroPower_1

The authors then keep the top seven features as the final input set.

5.2 Market Interpretation from SHAP

  • DK2_7 was the most influential feature overall.
  • Among neighboring-country variables, Germany (DE_1) had the strongest influence on Danish prices.
  • Among renewables, hydropower ranked above wind and solar.
  • Among exchange variables, ExGE_1 (exchange between DK1 and Germany) was most important.

5.3 Directional effects found by SHAP

  • For DK2_7:
    • lower historical DK2 prices tended to push predictions down,
    • higher historical DK2 prices tended to push predictions up.
  • For SE4_1:
    • lower values tended to increase predicted DK1 prices,
    • higher values tended to reduce them.
  • For Wind_1:
    • the paper identifies a threshold around 1000,
    • below this value, wind tended to reduce price,
    • above it, wind tended to increase price.
  • For ExGE_1:
    • below roughly 800, exchange with Germany increased price,
    • above roughly 1000, it decreased price.

5.4 What is useful here

  • This is one of the stronger parts of the paper.
  • The authors do not just rank features; they try to interpret market mechanisms.
  • Even if the interpretations are simplified, the workflow is useful:
    • global ranking,
    • local directional analysis,
    • threshold-style dependence plots.

6. Forecasting Results

6.1 Point Forecasting Results

The paper compares BPNN, LSTM, LSTNet, and their SHAP-selected variants.

Table: Point forecasting metrics

  • BPNN: RMSE = 8.35, MAPE = 13.02
  • LSTM: RMSE = 7.80, MAPE = 14.62
  • LSTNet: RMSE = 4.96, MAPE = 8.39
  • SHAP–BPNN: RMSE = 4.27, MAPE = 10.34
  • SHAP–LSTM: RMSE = 3.62, MAPE = 9.28
  • SHAP–LSTNet: RMSE = 2.35, MAPE = 5.39

Key takeaway

  • LSTNet was the best base model.
  • SHAP-based feature selection improved all three model families.
  • The best overall point forecasting model was SHAP–LSTNet.

6.2 Probabilistic Forecasting Results

The benchmark probabilistic models are SHAP–BPNN, SHAP–LSTM, and SHAP–LSTNet.

At confidence level 0.9:

  • SHAP–BPNN: PINAW = 22.22, PICP = 10.87, AL = 2.61
  • SHAP–LSTM: PINAW = 72.22, PICP = 32.74, AL = 0.88
  • SHAP–LSTNet: PINAW = 97.22, PICP = 35.79, AL = 0.41

At confidence level 0.8:

  • SHAP–BPNN: PINAW = 15.28, PICP = 7.42, AL = 2.25
  • SHAP–LSTM: PINAW = 44.44, PICP = 25.72, AL = 1.58
  • SHAP–LSTNet: PINAW = 80.56, PICP = 27.13, AL = 0.80

At confidence level 0.7:

  • SHAP–BPNN: PINAW = 9.72, PICP = 5.77, AL = 4.88
  • SHAP–LSTM: PINAW = 34.72, PICP = 17.42, AL = 1.85
  • SHAP–LSTNet: PINAW = 77.78, PICP = 24.13, AL = 1.14

Their conclusion from probabilistic results

  • The authors conclude that SHAP–LSTNet at 0.9 confidence is the best probabilistic model, mainly because it achieves the smallest average pinball loss.
  • They argue that narrow intervals alone are not desirable if they fail to cover the actual observations.

7. Validation Strategy

  • Forecasting validation: straightforward hold-out split
    • train on May–July 2019,
    • test on August 2019.
  • Point forecasting validation: RMSE and MAPE
  • Probabilistic validation: PICP, PINAW, AL
  • Interpretability validation: qualitative SHAP plots and market-based reasoning

What is missing

  • No rolling-origin evaluation
  • No walk-forward backtesting across multiple months or seasons
  • No repeated experiments across years
  • No robustness analysis under price spikes, regime shifts, or extreme events
  • No formal quantitative validation of explanations

8. My Thoughts, Criticisms & Unstated Limitations

Criticism 1: The dataset is too short for strong claims

  • The training window is only three months and the test window is only one month.
  • Electricity price forecasting is highly regime-sensitive.
  • A model that works over one August test month may not generalize across winter, fuel shocks, transmission events, or unusual renewable conditions.
  • This is a serious limitation if the paper is read as evidence of broad practical superiority.

Criticism 2: SHAP is used on top of a small-sample setup

  • SHAP rankings can be useful, but with limited data, feature-importance estimates can become unstable.
  • The paper does not test whether the selected top-seven feature set is stable across:
    • different train/test splits,
    • different random seeds,
    • or different model initializations.
  • So the feature-selection conclusions may be more fragile than the paper suggests.

Criticism 3: The probabilistic evaluation looks odd

  • The reported PICP values are surprisingly low relative to the stated confidence levels.
  • For example, at a 0.9 confidence level, a PICP of 35.79 is far below what one would expect if the intervals were calibrated in the usual sense.
  • That raises at least one of three possibilities:
    1. unusual scaling/definition,
    2. reporting inconsistency,
    3. poor calibration despite low pinball loss.
  • The paper does not fully unpack this tension.

Criticism 4: KDE after quantile prediction is a practical workaround, not a fully coherent probabilistic model

  • The framework first predicts quantiles and then reconstructs a density with KDE.
  • This can be useful, but it is a somewhat stitched-together pipeline.
  • It does not guarantee the same degree of internal probabilistic coherence as methods that directly learn a full conditional distribution.

Criticism 5: Baselines are weak and narrow

  • The comparison set is mainly BPNN, LSTM, and LSTNet variants.
  • That is not enough to establish state-of-the-art superiority in electricity price forecasting.
  • Stronger baselines could have included:
    • quantile gradient boosting,
    • quantile random forests,
    • LightGBM/XGBoost quantile models,
    • conformalized interval methods,
    • transformer-based time-series models,
    • modern probabilistic deep learning baselines.

Criticism 6: Interpretability is descriptive, not rigorously validated

  • The SHAP analysis is interesting and readable.
  • But the paper treats plausible feature plots as sufficient evidence of interpretability quality.
  • It does not test:
    • explanation stability,
    • explanation fidelity,
    • sensitivity to background distribution choice,
    • or whether the explanations remain consistent across retraining runs.

Criticism 7: No explicit treatment of quantile crossing

  • Since the method predicts multiple quantiles, quantile crossing is a natural concern.
  • The paper does not clearly discuss whether the predicted quantiles were constrained or post-processed to remain monotonic.
  • That matters if the quantiles are later fed into KDE.

Criticism 8: Market interpretation may be overly literal

  • The paper interprets SHAP dependence plots as if they reveal stable market thresholds, such as wind = 1000 or exchange = 800.
  • These thresholds may just reflect local patterns in this dataset rather than deep structural market truths.
  • The paper could have been more careful in separating:
    • data-specific empirical patterns,
    • from broadly generalizable market mechanisms.

9. What This Paper Does Well

  • It gives a clean and understandable pipeline from feature selection to probabilistic output.
  • It uses SHAP in a more meaningful way than many papers that only include one generic feature-importance plot.
  • It combines prediction and interpretation in a way that is easy to explain to readers.
  • It shows clearly that feature selection can materially improve forecasting accuracy.
  • It is a useful reference paper if your own work involves:
    • SHAP-based screening,
    • quantile forecasting,
    • probabilistic time-series modeling,
    • or energy-market XAI.

10. Where This Paper Sits in the Literature

This paper sits at the intersection of three threads:

  1. Electricity price forecasting
    • moving from classical statistical models toward deep learning.
  2. Probabilistic forecasting
    • shifting away from deterministic point prediction toward quantiles, intervals, and densities.
  3. Explainable AI for forecasting
    • using SHAP not just for post hoc explanation, but also as a feature-selection mechanism.

Its strongest identity is probably: > a hybrid XAI + probabilistic forecasting paper for electricity markets, where SHAP is used both to reduce dimensionality and to interpret price drivers.

11. Relevance to My Own Research / How I Could Use It

  • Useful as a citation for:
    • SHAP-based feature selection in energy forecasting,
    • probabilistic forecasting using quantile regression,
    • combining interpretability with deep temporal models,
    • nonparametric density reconstruction after quantile estimation.
  • Useful as a methodological stepping stone:
    • It provides a precedent for using SHAP before a deep forecasting model.
    • It supports the idea that reducing redundant inputs can improve both accuracy and interpretability.
  • Useful as a paper to critique in a literature review:
    • small dataset,
    • limited baselines,
    • weak probabilistic calibration discussion,
    • no rigorous XAI validation,
    • no stability analysis for selected features.
  • Useful positioning angle for my own work:

      • robust large-scale validation,
      • stronger probabilistic calibration checks,
      • richer baselines,
      • and formal explanation-quality evaluation.

13. Final Verdict

This is a useful and well-structured applied paper rather than a definitive state-of-the-art benchmark. Its main strength is not that it proves the best possible probabilistic electricity price forecaster, but that it presents a clear and intuitive SHAP + LSTNet + quantile/KDE pipeline that is easy to understand, easy to cite, and reasonably convincing as a proof of concept.

Its key message is: > SHAP-guided dimensionality reduction can improve deep probabilistic electricity price forecasting, while also making the model more interpretable.

Its main weakness is: > the experimental setup is too limited to fully justify strong general claims about robustness, calibration, and state-of-the-art performance.
