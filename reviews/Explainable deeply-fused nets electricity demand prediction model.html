Explainable deeply-fused nets electricity demand prediction model: Factoring climate predictors for accuracy and deeper insights with probabilistic confidence interval and point-based forecasts

Review of an Applied Energy study proposing an explainable deeply fused CNN-BILSTM model for daily electricity demand forecasting with bootstrap-based uncertainty quantification and SHAP analysis.
Published

March 13, 2026

  • Authors & Year: Sujan Ghimire, Mohanad S. AL-Musaylh, Thong Nguyen-Huy, Ravinesh C. Deo, Rajendra Acharya, David Casillas-Pérez, Zaher Mundher Yaseen, and Sancho Salcedo-Sanz (2025 issue; accepted 2024)
  • Venue: Applied Energy
  • DOI: 10.1016/j.apenergy.2024.124763
  • Core Topic: Explainable deep learning for daily electricity demand forecasting with both point predictions and 95% prediction intervals
  • Primary Methods: Deeply Fused Network (FNET), residual bootstrapping, SHAP
  • Study Region: Four substations in Southeast Queensland, Australia
  • Threat/Proximity Level: High relevance for explainable probabilistic load forecasting, especially where climate predictors and uncertainty intervals are central

2. Core Contributions, Novelty & Motivation

The Motivation (Why this study matters)

The paper starts from a very practical power-systems problem: electricity demand is nonlinear, climate-sensitive, and inherently uncertain. Pure point forecasting is not enough for operational decision-making, because operators, aggregators, and traders also need a sense of forecast uncertainty. The authors therefore position their work around two needs: 1. Higher predictive accuracy for daily electricity demand. 2. Better uncertainty quantification and interpretability for real-world energy decision-making.

The paper explicitly argues that demand models should not only predict future load accurately, but should also quantify uncertainty and explain which factors drive those forecasts. This is framed as important for electricity market planning, grid operation, demand response, and trading.

Their claim to fame / novelty

The main novelty is the adaptation of Deeply Fused Nets (FNET), originally used in image/text domains, to a time-series electricity demand forecasting setting. The authors argue that this is the first use of this specific deep fusion idea for electricity demand forecasting, and they extend it with: - CNN + BILSTM deep fusion - Residual bootstrapping for 95% prediction intervals - SHAP analysis for explainability

So the novelty is not just “another hybrid CNN-LSTM,” but specifically a deep fusion architecture where intermediate representations from multiple branches are fused repeatedly, instead of only at the final stage. The study also adds a probabilistic layer via bootstrap intervals and an interpretability layer via SHAP.

Why the explainability part is important here

The explainability contribution is not treated as decorative. The authors use SHAP to make the model more transparent and to identify the role of lagged demand and climate predictors in the final forecast. Their results suggest that historical demand and evapotranspiration are among the most influential predictors, with SHAP also revealing interaction effects, especially between lagged demand and evapotranspiration.

Why the uncertainty part is important here

Instead of assuming a fixed parametric output distribution, the study uses bootstrap residual resampling to generate 95% prediction intervals. This is important because electricity demand is chaotic and nonstationary, and the authors explicitly argue that making strong distributional assumptions may distort risk estimates.

3. Methodology & Technical Details

3.1 Dataset / testbed

The study uses electricity demand data from four substations in Southeast Queensland, Australia: - Annerley - Heathwood - Laidley - Zillmere

The raw dataset spans 01/07/2011 to 30/06/2021, with 280,560 measurements sampled at 30-minute intervals, later downsampled to daily resolution. This results in a 10-year daily forecasting problem.

3.2 Input variables

The model does not rely on lagged demand alone. It combines historical electricity demand with 10 climate variables from the SILO database. These include: - Maximum temperature - Minimum temperature - Vapour pressure - Vapour pressure deficit - Synthetic evaporation - Solar radiation - Relative humidity at maximum temperature - Relative humidity at minimum temperature - Evapotranspiration - Mean sea level pressure

The lag structure is chosen using PACF and Mutual Information Function (MIF) analysis. The chosen lag depths differ by substation: - Annerley: 4 lag days - Heathwood: 6 lag days - Laidley: 5 lag days - Zillmere: 6 lag days

This is one of the better methodological choices in the paper, because the lag structure is not arbitrarily imposed.

3.3 Preprocessing and data split

The authors use min-max normalization and split the data into: - 90% training/validation: 01/07/2011 to 30/06/2020 - 10% testing: 01/07/2020 to 30/06/2021

Within the training portion, 20% is used as a validation set for hyperparameter tuning.

3.4 The proposed model

The proposed model is a Deeply Fused Network (FNET) built from: - 1D CNN - BILSTM - repeated deep fusion of intermediate representations

Key architectural details include: - CNN filters ranging from 32 to 128 - a 4-layer BILSTM branch - a final single BILSTM regression layer - SeLU activation in the CNN branch - ReLU activation in the dense layer - Adam optimizer

The authors argue that deep fusion offers several benefits over shallow fusion: - better information flow, - learning at intermediate representation levels, - more representative feature embeddings, - extraction of multi-scale features.

3.5 Baselines

The FNET model is compared against: - BILSTM - LSTMCNN - DNN - MLP - MARS - KRR - GPR

This gives a reasonably broad comparison across both shallow and deep learning baselines, although there are still important omissions, discussed later.

3.6 Uncertainty quantification

For interval forecasting, the authors use residual bootstrapping: - 95% confidence intervals - 1000 bootstrap samples

The paper explicitly prefers bootstrap-based intervals because they do not require strong prior assumptions about the underlying output distribution.

3.7 Explainability

The paper uses SHAP to: - rank feature importance, - interpret marginal feature effects, - analyse interactions between predictors, - inspect how climate variables and lagged demand affect model output.

4. Validation Strategy

4.1 Point forecast validation

The deterministic evaluation includes multiple metrics, including: - Correlation coefficient (r) - RMSE - MAE - additional efficiency and bias-related metrics later in the paper, such as Willmott’s Index, Nash–Sutcliffe efficiency, Legates–McCabe index, KGE, GPI, and CPI.

This is a strength of the study: it does not rely on a single accuracy measure.

4.2 Probabilistic validation

The prediction intervals are evaluated with several uncertainty metrics, including: - PICP - MPIW - PINAW - ARIL - Winkler Score - CRPS - F-value

This is a strong point. The paper treats uncertainty quality as multi-dimensional rather than reducing it to just one interval metric.

4.3 Explainability validation

The XAI validation is primarily qualitative and domain-informed. The authors use SHAP plots to interpret: - the dominance of lagged load, - the importance of evapotranspiration, - interaction effects between lagged demand and climate drivers.

This gives meaningful interpretive insight, but it is not a formal XAI evaluation in the robustness/fidelity sense.

5. Key Results

5.1 Deterministic forecasting performance

The paper reports that FNET consistently performs best or among the best across the four substations and across multiple deterministic metrics.

A few concrete examples: - Annerley: FNET achieved r = 0.974, RMSE = 15.136 MW, MAE = 11.641 MW - On the same substation, the nearest deep baselines were slightly worse: - LSTMCNN: r = 0.967, RMSE = 16.233 MW, MAE = 12.482 MW - BILSTM: r = 0.965, RMSE = 16.481 MW, MAE = 12.484 MW - Weakest Annerley performers included: - KRR: r = 0.926, RMSE = 23.480 MW, MAE = 17.593 MW - MARS: r = 0.936, RMSE = 21.984 MW, MAE = 17.219 MW - MLP: r = 0.950, RMSE = 19.579 MW, MAE = 14.889 MW

For Laidley, FNET again performs strongly: - FNET: r = 0.963, RMSE = 13.038 MW, MAE = 10.266 MW - This is better than: - BILSTM: r = 0.957, RMSE = 14.449 MW, MAE = 11.488 MW - LSTMCNN: r = 0.961, RMSE = 14.370 MW, MAE = 11.425 MW

Across the paper’s wider set of metrics, the proposed FNET is repeatedly ranked first or near first, while shallow baselines such as MLP, KRR, and MARS tend to perform worst.

5.2 Additional performance metrics

The paper also shows strong FNET results on: - Willmott’s Index - Nash–Sutcliffe coefficient - Legates & McCabe index - APB - KGE - GPI - CPI

For example, at Laidley, FNET achieves: - EWI ≈ 0.891 - ENS ≈ 0.859 - ELM ≈ 0.603

The authors use these broader metrics to argue that FNET is not just slightly better in one narrow sense, but more robust overall.

5.3 Probabilistic forecasting performance

The probabilistic evaluation also favours FNET. The paper states that the model’s prediction intervals perform well across multiple uncertainty metrics and that the empirical error distribution and ECDF analyses show clearer separation in favour of FNET. The FNET intervals appear to maintain stronger performance when judged jointly by coverage and width, rather than only one of these.

The broader takeaway is that the proposed architecture is not only a better point forecaster, but also a more useful uncertainty-aware forecaster.

5.4 Explainability findings

The SHAP analysis identifies lagged electricity demand and evapotranspiration as especially influential variables. A major interpretive insight is that evapotranspiration generally contributes positively, but its contribution becomes stronger when lagged demand is high. In other words, the model is not simply learning isolated one-feature effects; SHAP suggests interaction structure between demand history and climate variables.

This is useful because it shows the model is not behaving as a totally opaque black box. The paper’s interpretation implies the network is capturing a meaningful coupling between thermal/climatic conditions and consumption patterns.

5.5 Computational cost

One downside is computational efficiency. The paper explicitly states that FNET is less computationally efficient than the benchmark models. However, it also argues that once trained, the model can remain in service for an extended period and that testing time is under one minute, which makes deployment still practical for real applications.

6. What the Paper Does Well

6.1 Strong methodological breadth

This is one of the stronger papers in the space because it combines: - advanced hybrid deep learning, - probabilistic uncertainty estimation, - multiple deterministic and probabilistic evaluation metrics, - and explainability.

A lot of studies do only one or two of these.

6.2 Good feature engineering logic

The use of PACF and MIF to select lag structure is a sound step and gives the model design more credibility than arbitrary lag selection.

6.3 Sensible uncertainty method

Bootstrap residual intervals are a good fit for this application because they avoid strong parametric output assumptions. That is a sensible design choice for a volatile real-world demand signal.

6.4 Explainability is tied to domain meaning

The SHAP analysis is not merely decorative. It identifies influential climate variables and interaction effects that can plausibly matter in real electricity demand dynamics.

6.5 Broad evaluation

The paper evaluates performance from many angles, which makes its conclusion more convincing than papers that rely only on RMSE or only on one interval metric.

7. My Thoughts, Criticisms & Unstated Limitations

Criticism 1: “Explainable” does not mean rigorously validated explainability

The paper uses SHAP well for qualitative interpretation, but it does not formally evaluate explanation quality using metrics such as: - explanation stability, - robustness, - fidelity, - sensitivity to perturbations, - or agreement across runs/background choices.

So although the SHAP figures are insightful, the study does not prove that the explanations themselves are stable or trustworthy in a stricter XAI sense.

Criticism 2: Residual bootstrap intervals are practical, but still limited

Bootstrap intervals are attractive because they are distribution-light, but they are still built on residual behaviour learned from past model errors. That means they may underperform under structural change, abrupt demand regime shifts, or unseen climate-demand patterns. The paper does not deeply interrogate whether residual bootstrap remains well calibrated under distribution shift.

Criticism 3: The benchmark set is broad, but not cutting-edge

The comparison is fair across several classical and hybrid baselines, but from a current forecasting perspective, the paper still omits stronger modern sequence models and probabilistic methods, for example: - quantile neural networks, - transformer-based forecasters, - mixture density approaches, - conformal prediction baselines, - distributional deep learning alternatives.

So while FNET beats the included baselines, the broader state-of-the-art comparison remains incomplete.

Criticism 4: Limited external generalization

All experiments are conducted on four substations in one regional context in Queensland. That is useful, but it does leave open the question of whether the same gains hold across: - different countries, - different climates, - larger transmission-level systems, - more abrupt socio-economic demand patterns, - or higher renewable penetration settings.

Criticism 5: Interpretability is still mostly feature attribution, not mechanistic understanding

The SHAP analysis tells us which features matter and how they trend, but it still does not fully reveal the internal mechanics of the deep fused representation. So the model becomes more interpretable, but not truly transparent in a structural sense.

Criticism 6: Computational cost matters more than the paper admits

The authors acknowledge that FNET is slower than alternatives. That is important. In operational environments where models must be retrained frequently, or where many substations/regions must be modeled simultaneously, this cost can become a real deployment bottleneck.

8. Positioning This Paper in the Literature

This paper is best positioned as a high-quality hybrid deep learning paper that sits at the intersection of: - load forecasting, - uncertainty quantification, - and post-hoc interpretability.

Its biggest contribution is not that it invents uncertainty quantification or SHAP for forecasting, but that it integrates: 1. a deep fusion architecture, 2. bootstrap-based interval prediction, 3. and SHAP-based interpretation into one forecasting workflow.

Relative to weaker forecasting papers, it is stronger because it goes beyond point accuracy. Relative to cutting-edge probabilistic forecasting research, it is still somewhat transitional, because its uncertainty layer is bootstrap-based rather than intrinsically distributional or conformal.

9. Relevance to My Own Research / Why This Paper Matters

This paper is highly relevant if my research is about: - interpretable probabilistic demand forecasting, - climate-aware load prediction, - comparing point vs interval forecasting performance, - integrating SHAP into forecasting pipelines, - or arguing for more transparent forecasting tools in energy systems.

It is especially useful as a citation for the claim that accurate load prediction alone is insufficient and that uncertainty and interpretability must also be incorporated.

It is also useful as a methodological reference if I want to justify: - hybrid CNN-BILSTM forecasting, - bootstrap-based prediction intervals, - climate feature integration, - or SHAP analysis of load drivers.

10. Action Items & Integration

11. Bottom-Line Takeaway

This is a strong and relevant paper. Its main achievement is showing that a deeply fused CNN-BILSTM model, augmented with bootstrap prediction intervals and SHAP explanations, can outperform a wide set of classical and neural baselines for daily electricity demand forecasting. The paper is methodologically richer than many load forecasting studies because it treats forecasting as a three-part problem: accuracy, uncertainty, and interpretability.

Its main weaknesses are that the explainability is validated only qualitatively, the probabilistic method is still residual-bootstrap rather than a more modern intrinsically probabilistic framework, and the benchmark set does not fully represent the strongest current state of the art. Even so, it is a very useful paper and a strong reference point for any work on explainable probabilistic energy forecasting.
